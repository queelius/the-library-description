# Implications: What the Cut Does to Human Concepts

The bedrock, the postulate, and finitude are established (`foundations.md`). This document traces
what they *mean* when taken seriously: what they do to the human concepts that come under pressure
in Part III. The framing has shifted with the re-foundation, from "what multiplicity does to each
concept" to "each concept is a cut."

Each implication is traceable. The reader can always ask "which of bedrock, finitude, or the
postulate does this rest on?" The answers are noted, in the new vocabulary (the old "Moves I, II"
are gone; quantum branching is a familiar landmark, not a step).

---

## The Self as a Cut

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate.

The simplest dissolution. Once the inversion is taken seriously, "you" is a boundary drawn around a
region of structure for navigation: a short local program, the brain's self-model. The same region
can be carved out by many different boundaries, and the choice of boundary is the observer's, not
the structure's.

In Worldlines the self was dissolved temporally: the four-dimensional worm has the five-year-old
and the eighty-year-old as equally real cross-sections, and "I" is what a cross-section calls
itself. This book dissolves the self as a carving. "I" is a cut, parallel to the index; self and
index dissolve together because they are one move at two levels (see `index.md`).

**What survives:** the region. The configurations of structure that have, at their coordinates, the
features you call your mind, are real. What does not survive is the claim that the boundary around
them was in the structure rather than in your interface to it.

**What this means for action:** the same as Worldlines. Your action is at its coordinates. You act
here; the configuration "you who acts here" is one carving among many, and you do not coordinate
across carvings.

## Free Will and the Chooser

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate (quantum branching as a familiar landmark).

Worldlines argued that free will in the block universe is what choice looks like from the inside of
a fixed structure; compatibilism survives, and the felt openness is a feature of the worldline, not
a window onto an open future.

This book refines it. The chooser is a cut. The plainest reading of branching says "could have
chosen otherwise" is literally true (there are carvings in which the choice went the other way), and
that this gives the felt openness no extra metaphysical weight. The compatibilist response is
reinforced, not overturned: you feel the choice, the structure contains the alternatives, and the
feeling and the structure are both real and do not conflict. The book does not lean on the branches
being real to make this point; even read purely as carvings, the conclusion holds.

**The first-person sequence.** Under indexical assumptions the book does not adopt, the first-person
sequence might continue along non-terminating carvings: the quantum-immortality thread. The book
takes it seriously as the shape of a problem and explicitly declines to assert it (under the
postulate it cannot even claim the relevant carvings are real). It is a labeled implication, not a
result.

## Mortality and the Asymmetry

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate. (No observer-measure assumption is adopted; the indexical
claims below are one reading, not a result.)

In Worldlines, death is the boundary of the four-dimensional worm; the suffering at every point is
permanent at its coordinates. This book refines the picture: the boundary called death is a cut.
Some carvings of the pattern you call yourself terminate; some do not, or the termination is
delayed, or the continuation is in a carving of vanishing weight. This produces an asymmetry.

**Third-person death:** real. Your loved ones lose you in their carving; the grief is at their
coordinates; the braid has a specific endpoint there. This is not softened by other carvings in
which the braid continues.

**First-person death:** under quantum-immortality-style assumptions the book does not adopt, the
first-person sequence has continuations where the termination did not occur. From the outside you
died; from the inside you continued. The book refuses to assert this as real, on two grounds: it
adopts no indexical assumption, and under the postulate it does not claim the carvings exist.

**The ethics of the asymmetry.** This is one of the harder passages in Part III. The third-person
grief in this carving is real; the first-person presence in others does not console it. The chapter
sits with the difficulty rather than resolving it, and does not claim quantum immortality is
consoling. It is the shape of the problem, not its solution.

## Love and the Braid

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate (quantum branching as a familiar landmark).

Worldlines described love as two worldlines intertwined, shaping each other's structure; the braid
is a feature of the manifold, real at its coordinates. This book shows the braid as a carving we
maintain. The two patterns you call yourself and another are braided wherever they are braided, and
each instantiation is real at its coordinates. The braid is not less because it is multiple; it is a
carving we keep, and the conventions do not maintain themselves.

This deepens rather than dilutes the compassion argument. The care you provide is real at every
coordinate it exists.

**Where this gets harder.** Some carvings hold a braid that ended in cruelty; some hold one that
ended in care; some hold none. The structure ranks none above the others. The book is honest: the
cruelty exists at its coordinates as much as the kindness does. The chosen response is to act in
*this* carving with care, knowing other carvings hold other acts and that you do not control them.
This is the analog of Worldlines' "you cannot rewrite the coordinates of your past carelessness": it
exists in many carvings, so does the present care, and neither cancels the other. You act here.

## Memory

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate.

Worldlines reframed memory as a cross-reference, not a record: a configuration at one set of
coordinates that partially and lossily mirrors a configuration at another. This book extends it.
Your memory cross-references coordinates within your carving; the events it mirrors exist at their
coordinates, and similar-but-different versions exist in carvings that diverged from yours. "The
true memory" is well-defined within your carving and ill-defined across carvings (which carving's
original?). The notion of a single privileged original is itself a cut. For practical purposes "the
true memory" means your carving's coordinates, and memory remains a usable guide, situated rather
than undermined.

## Boltzmann Observers and the Past

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate (the speed prior and other observer-measure machinery are
discussed, not adopted).

The framework permits a problem: under unweighted observer-counting, most observer-moments matching
your self-description may be transient fluctuations rather than embedded beings with continuous
histories. The book treats this honestly. The worry is mathematically real (in sufficiently
long-lived, bounded systems, fluctuation observer-states are inevitable and can outnumber ordinary
ones). The standard responses (reference-class restriction, the speed prior, cosmological arguments)
each carry a cost, and the book adopts none of them as a resolution; it discusses them in Chapter 9
and treats the Boltzmann hypothesis as one of many uncomfortable carvings the framework permits.

Under the re-foundation the worry is reframed but not removed. "Your past may not be real" becomes
"the past is a cut": the question is whether the local structure of this configuration is better
carved as a long lawful history or as a momentary fluctuation, and finitude (hierarchical
decomposability, see `index.md`) bears on which carving a working observer can sustain. The grammar
shifts; the worry remains. The book does not engineer it away. The reader sits with the uncertainty,
and the chosen response of Part III is what follows.

## History and Progress

**Cascade:** Bedrock + the postulate.

Worldlines dissolved the Whig narrative: the block universe contains all events, and "things got
better" is a statement about later coordinates having features we prefer. This book extends it. The
totality contains every history as a carving: the war averted and the war not averted, the suffering
that occurred and the suffering that did not. The structure singles out no actual history as the
real one; from any one carving, history feels like the path taken; the structure takes no path. This
deepens the dissolution rather than restoring narrative. The chosen response, to act in this carving
for kindness, is one configuration among many; we act in ours.

## Purpose and the Choice of Care

**Cascade:** the chosen response, not derived from bedrock, finitude, or the postulate.

Worldlines argued that purpose, if it survives the block universe, must mean something other than
shaping the future: the aesthetic character of your worldline, a worldline that contains care being
structurally different from one that contains cruelty. This book extends it. The pattern you call
yourself holds many carvings; some contain care, some cruelty, some neither; the structural
difference between a carving with care and one without is real at every coordinate where care
exists.

**The choice of care.** It is not derived from the mathematics. The mathematics provides the
structure; the choice to act with care in this carving is the author's preference, stated honestly
from within the room the mathematics leaves open, and the reader is invited to make it or refuse it.
What the book argues: care is the only warmth in an indifferent structure, the structure does not
prefer it, and providing it is one of the few cuts you have direct access to (most cuts are
existence-only; you live somewhere, but you do not choose where). This is the load-bearing claim of
the closing, the same claim Worldlines made: we are the only things in the structure that can care,
and the caring is the only warmth, made here the held cut.

---

## What the Implications Do Not Include

The book is careful not to overreach. Claims the framework does *not* support:

- **It does not prove quantum immortality.** It shows quantum immortality is the shape of a problem
  about indexical first-person continuation, under assumptions the book takes seriously without
  adopting; under the postulate it does not even assert the relevant carvings are real.
- **It does not prove you are not a Boltzmann observer.** The framework permits the possibility and
  the book does not engineer it away; the technical responses are surveyed, none adopted.
- **It does not prove the postulate.** "No cut is privileged" is the single interpretive commitment,
  labeled where it enters; the reader can refuse it and the bedrock survives.
- **It does not prove that care matters.** It shows the choice of care is one of the cuts you have
  access to and that the structure does not forbid it. The case for care is not derived from the
  math; it is chosen.

The discipline is the same throughout. The mathematics provides the structure; finitude explains the
bias; the postulate gives the indifference; the implications follow from the cascade; the chosen
response is yours.
