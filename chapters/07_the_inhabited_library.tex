\noindent \textsc{Chapter 6 made the slip.} The universal prior is now a measure on realities, not just on hypotheses. The Library contains every computable structure as a real thing.

What follows is the picture the slip gives you, an older question the picture sharpens, and the honest limits of any of it.

\section*{What the postulate gives}

With the postulate taken, the picture becomes:

The Library contains every computable reality. The universal prior $M$ is a measure on it. Your data restricts you to the subset of realities consistent with what you have observed. The measure on that subset describes which reality is yours, weighted by how plausible each candidate is.

You exist at many indices in the Library, in this picture. The pattern that constitutes your situation is instantiated wherever the structure of your situation occurs. Most of those instantiations are in universes with simple laws. A vanishingly small number are in universes whose laws are pathological. The universal prior tells you the proportions.

The question \emph{where am I?} becomes measure-theoretic. It does not have a unique answer. It has a distribution.

\section*{A note on time}

A reader will want to ask about time. The Library is static, a timeless totality (Chapter 6); but the universe you inhabit plainly has time in it, change, before and after, a present that seems to move. How does a static structure account for that?

The same way the block universe does. Computation does not require a flowing time any more than spacetime does. A program's execution is not a happening; it is a static object, the full trace of its configurations, indexed by a step counter. Step $5$ does not \emph{become} step $6$; both are present in the trace, immutable, the way every moment of a worldline is present in the manifold. ``Running'' is how an observer embedded in the trace reads it, not something the trace does. Time inside a computation is a logical index over static configurations, a coordinate, not a current. And the time you feel passing is, as in \textit{Worldlines}, a feature of your vantage from inside one of those configurations, not a motion of the whole. The Library holds the index and every structure it labels at once, unmoving. We will not linger on this; it is the same eternalism, one level out.

\section*{An older question}

A reader who has followed this far may have an older question waiting. \emph{Why is there something rather than nothing?}

The question seems to assume that nothing is the simpler option. The chapter's tools do not directly settle this. Nothing, taken seriously, is the empty set: every possibility excluded. Like the universal set, it is informationally cheap. Both extremes have low description length. They are, in a sense, complements of the same kind of thing.

So the cut principle does not, by itself, favor either nothing or something. Both are cheap.

What does favor something: we observe it.

You are reading these words. Something exists. This is not a metaphysical assumption; it is the data. Any theory of reality that predicts no existence at all is immediately falsified by the existence of the observer. The nothing-hypothesis is excluded by induction on the only sample available, which is the entirety of what anyone has ever observed.

The induction operates one level above the within-universe inductions of earlier chapters. We are not asking which universe is ours within the Library. We are asking which kind of generator generates realities at all. The nothing-generator generates none. We are in a reality. The nothing-generator is empirically excluded.

There is also a coherence question. Is \emph{nothing} a position one could coherently hold?

To say nothing exists is to say no possibilities are realized. The claim presupposes a domain of possibilities from which the realized ones are absent. But if nothing exists, no domain exists either. There are no possibilities to be absent. The very concept of an excluded possibility seems to require a background of possibilities to exclude from.

The concept of nothing may quietly require the concept of something to mean anything at all. It may not pick out a coherent position so much as the limit of a concept that breaks down before reaching it.

The book does not resolve this. The note is here because it is honest. The cut principle does not by itself rule out nothing on simplicity grounds; observation does. And on close inspection, nothing may not be a coherent option at all.

\section*{The contingency, and where it goes}

There is a harder question hiding behind the first, and it is the one that actually drives people to theology. Grant that something exists. Why \emph{this}? Why these laws, this universe, this arrangement and not another? For any particular system there is no answer from inside it. Its existence is a brute fact, contingent, resting on nothing further. That is hard to accept, harder than it first looks, and the mind reaches for a necessary ground: a reason the world had to be as it is. Historically the reach has landed on a necessary being: something that exists of itself and accounts for the rest.

The Library does not supply a necessary being, but it does change the question. \emph{Why this and not that?} is a question about a selection, and the all-realities ontology makes no selection. It is the universal set, the option that takes everything, the complement of the empty set. There is no chosen system whose choosing needs explaining, because nothing was chosen. The contingency that felt unbearable, \emph{why these laws and not others}, simply dissolves: every system is in the Library, and you are at one index, for no reason the structure can give.

This is not a resolution. It is a relocation, and a shrinking. A particular universe is radically contingent, a brute fact the size of a world. The totality is the least contingent thing on offer, because it selects nothing, and, as the coherence note just suggested, it may be the one option that cannot be coherently denied. The contingency does not vanish. It contracts to the thinnest residue: that there is a space of possibility at all. The weighting over that space is not a further mystery; for a finite predictor it is what Kraft forces (Chapter 4), and reading it as a fact about reality rather than about the predictor is the one postulate the previous chapter named, that no cut is privileged. What does not dissolve is that there is anything to weight at all, a brute fact even the totality cannot talk its way out of, and the book does not pretend that it can.

So where theology offered a necessary mind, the framework offers something colder and smaller: a near-necessary structure that chose nothing and is no one. That substitution is the whole of this book in miniature. Not a someone at the ground of things who meant it, but a measure, indifferent, that did not.

\section*{What if you refuse}

The book takes the postulate. The reader does not have to.

Refuse it, and the universal prior remains exactly what Part I built: the right epistemic prior. Cox's theorem, Kraft's inequality, Solomonoff's dominance theorem all still apply, and the mathematics of optimal learning is unchanged. What you give up is only the ontological reading. The prior tells you how to believe, not what exists; there is one world, and $M$ is how a finite reasoner should bet on it. Everything in Part I stands. (If you go further and doubt even the computability frame, the claim that physical reality is the kind of thing $M$ describes at all, you keep less still, but the bedrock as a theory of belief is untouched, because it never needed the world to be computable to be the right way to reason.)

Many of the implications in Part III survive the refusal. The temporal dissolution of the unitary self (the way \textit{Worldlines} treated it) does not need the postulate. The discipline of attention, the practice of care, the response to indifference: all of these survive without the larger picture.

What does need the postulate is the multiplicity itself: quantum immortality, the dissolution of the self \emph{across} carvings, the Boltzmann problem. These rest on the refusal to privilege one world, and a reader who keeps that one world is free of them.

The book marks which conclusions rest on which footing. The cascade can always be unwound: a claim that needs the postulate is flagged as such, a claim that follows from bedrock alone is unmarked because it is settled, and the reader can trace any conclusion to its footing and refuse the footing if the conclusion is unwelcome.

This is the structure of honesty the book commits to.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

The slip has been made and its picture has been drawn. The Library has been read ontologically. Multiplicity, in this picture, is a structural fact rather than a thought experiment.

The next chapter pauses on a reassurance before the book turns to what the picture means. Quantum mechanics, on its plainest reading, already branches: a smaller multiplicity sitting inside the larger one, in physics we have already confirmed. It is not a second, independent route, since the quantum branches are themselves a region of the Library. It is the same picture met in the one place physics has already taken us, and it is where the Born measure enters, the measure this book reads as the universal prior in other clothes, the same bookkeeping of cuts.

The Library, already inside our own physics.
