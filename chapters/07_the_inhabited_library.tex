\chapterepigraph
{The world is everything that is the case.}
{Ludwig Wittgenstein, \textit{Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus} (1921)}

\noindent \textsc{Chapter 6 exposed the slip} and put one refusal in its place: no cut is privileged, including the cut that calls one system \emph{the world}. Take the refusal, and two pictures remain. This chapter draws them, names the gap they share, and then follows them to an older question.

\section*{What the postulate gives, and does not}

In Picture A, program-output space is contemplated as a total object, surveyed from outside. In Picture B, you are situated inside it, with finite data and a posterior over candidate programs. In both, $M$ does the same work: it weights programs whose outputs begin with your data. Neither picture, by itself, makes every output a real world. Picture A read ontically would; that reading is an optional addition, not a consequence of anything so far.

What the two pictures share is the postulate's shape. Figure~\ref{fig:two-carvings} draws it: one field, carved twice, with nothing in the field to mark either carving as its own. A world is drawn the same way. ``This system, these laws, this history'' is a boundary you bring to the structure, and a different observer, or you with a different purpose, could draw another boundary straight across it. Nothing in the structure would register the redrawing as an error.

Your current data can be compatible with many programs: some encode simple lawful histories, some stranger continuations, some a momentary matching state. That is ordinary Bayesian underdetermination, and $M$ is built for it.

The question \emph{where am I?} now splits. \emph{Which programs best predict my next data?} $M$ supplies a posterior answer, relative to $U$. \emph{Which observer occurrence is me?} Here the framework falls silent, and the silence has a shape.

Call it \emph{the missing occurrence measure}. $M$ weights candidate programs and output prefixes. Nothing in the construction says what counts as an observer occurrence, whether occurrences exist, or how first-person weight is spread among them. Supplying that takes further rules: the self-sampling and self-indication assumptions, xerographic distributions, and their relatives, which Chapter 8 and Appendix~\ref{app:anthropics} take up. The book adopts none of them.

\section*{A note on time}

A reader will want to ask about time. Picture A presents program-output space statically; but the universe you inhabit plainly has time in it, change, before and after, a present that seems to move. How can a static description represent that?

One can represent an execution by its complete trace, indexed by a step counter. Step $5$ and step $6$ are both parts of that mathematical object. On the block-universe analogy, ``running'' is the embedded order in which configurations relate, not a motion of the whole representation. This is a feature of Picture A, not a result that settles the metaphysics of time.

\section*{An older question}

A reader who has followed this far may have an older question waiting. \emph{Why is there something rather than nothing?}

The question seems to assume that nothing is the simpler option. The chapter's tools do not directly settle this. Nothing, taken seriously, is the empty set: every possibility excluded. Like the universal set, it is informationally cheap. Both extremes have low description length. They are, in a sense, complements of the same kind of thing.

So the cut principle does not, by itself, favor either nothing or something. Both are cheap.

What does favor something: we observe it.

You are reading these words. Something exists. This is not a metaphysical assumption; it is the data. Any theory of reality that predicts no existence at all is refuted by the existence of the observer. The nothing-hypothesis is not weighed and found unlikely; it is contradicted, once, by the only sample anyone has ever had.

This is a refutation, not an induction, and it is a thin one. The fact of experience contradicts the proposition that there is no experience at all. It does not identify a generator, select Picture A, or supply a probability distribution over ontologies.

There is also a coherence question. Is \emph{nothing} a position one could coherently hold?

To say nothing exists is to say no possibilities are realized. The claim presupposes a domain of possibilities from which the realized ones are absent. But if nothing exists, no domain exists either. There are no possibilities to be absent. The very concept of an excluded possibility seems to require a background of possibilities to exclude from.

The concept of nothing may quietly require the concept of something to mean anything at all. It may not pick out a coherent position so much as the limit of a concept that breaks down before reaching it.

The book does not resolve this. The cut principle does not by itself rule out nothing on simplicity grounds; observation does. And on close inspection, nothing may not be a coherent option at all.

\section*{The contingency, and where it goes}

There is a harder question hiding behind the first, and it is the one that actually drives people to theology. Grant that something exists. Why \emph{this}? Why these laws, this universe, this arrangement and not another? For any particular system there is no answer from inside it. Its existence is a brute fact, contingent, resting on nothing further. That is hard to accept, harder than it first looks, and the mind reaches for a necessary ground: a reason the world had to be as it is. Historically the reach has landed on a necessary being: something that exists of itself and accounts for the rest.

The Library does not supply a necessary being, but Picture A changes the question. \emph{Why this and not that?} becomes a question about why one described system, one cut among many, is selected as actual. An all-realities ontology would decline that selection. Picture B leaves the ordinary empirical question intact: why do these data follow these laws, and which candidate program will continue to fit them?

This is not a resolution. Under Picture A it relocates contingency from a selected world to the status of the whole possibility space. Under Picture B it leaves a situated observer comparing fallible models. $M$'s weighting does not close the question. What remains is both the old empirical question and the deeper fact that there is anything to describe at all.

So where theology offered a necessary mind, this framework offers no substitute with comparable authority. It offers a space of computable descriptions, a powerful predictor over them, and an interpretive postulate about cuts. None is a someone at the ground of things, and none closes the question of why there is this world.

\section*{What if you refuse}

The book takes the postulate. The reader does not have to.

Refuse it, and Part I remains: Bayes' theorem, the fair-bit universal construction, Kraft's inequality, dominance, convergence, and machine-relative invariance, each with its stated scope. $M$ stays what Part I made it, a distinguished epistemic prior and never the uniquely required one.

Most of Chapter 2's inversion survives as well. Generation is still cheap: one short program still dovetails every candidate. Indexing is still expensive: picking out \emph{this} still costs what it cost before. And still no computable procedure sorts the Library by how cheaply each thing can be said; no algorithm will hand you an item's complexity or its shortest description. Those were facts before Chapter 6 asked for anything, and refusing a postulate does not unprove them. What you give up is one claim: that no decomposition is the correct one. You may hold that some carving is the structure's own, that the joints are there and a good cut finds them. Refusal does not force one-world realism either; many metaphysical positions remain available. If you also doubt the frame of computable descriptions, which Chapter 6 chose rather than proved, the mathematics still holds as mathematics, while its application to nature narrows.

Many of the implications in Part III survive the refusal. The temporal dissolution of the unitary self (the way \textit{Worldlines} treated it) does not need the postulate. The discipline of attention, the practice of care, the response to indifference: all of these survive without the larger picture.

Some conclusions need more than the postulate, and the book will say so when they arrive. Ontic multiplicity needs Picture A read ontically, or another ontology. Quantum immortality needs Everett, an identity rule, and a self-location rule; it needs the missing occurrence measure supplied. Boltzmann-observer conclusions need a cosmology and, again, the missing occurrence measure.

The book marks which conclusions rest on which footing, and there are five: theorem, construction, finitude, postulate, optional additions. A theorem holds under its stated hypotheses. A construction holds once you fix a universal machine and fair program bits. Finitude is the practical fact that a bounded observer must use local models. The postulate is the one refusal asked of you, in Chapter 6. The optional additions (Picture A read ontically, Everett, an identity rule, a self-location rule, a cosmology) are named wherever a conclusion would need them, and the book adopts none of them. The cascade can always be unwound: you can trace any conclusion to its footing and refuse the footing if the conclusion is unwelcome.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

Two pictures have been drawn, and the gap they share has a name. Picture A permits an ontological multiplicity; Picture B remains a posterior over candidate programs. Neither is the view from nowhere, and neither supplies the missing occurrence measure.

Quantum mechanics, read one particular way, offers a multiplicity of its own.
