\chapter{The Library}

\chapterepigraph
{The universe (which others call the Library) is composed of an indefinite, perhaps infinite number of hexagonal galleries.}
{Jorge Luis Borges, ``The Library of Babel'' (1941), trans.\ Hurley}

\noindent \textsc{In 1941, Jorge Luis Borges} described a library.

The library contains every possible book, in the one size it stocks: 410 pages, 40 lines per page, 80 characters per line, drawn from an alphabet of 25 symbols. The total number of distinct books is finite but enormous, roughly $25^{1{,}312{,}000}$.\footnote{This is a real number. It is finite. It is also vastly larger than the number of atoms in the observable universe, which is about $10^{80}$. Borges' narrator was uncertain whether the library was infinite, and guessed that it might be unlimited but periodic, the same volumes repeating in the same disorder. The mathematics settles a narrower question. However far the shelves run, the number of distinct books is finite.}

Most of the books are gibberish. Long runs of meaningless characters. Page after page of the letter $m$. A book that is the letter $e$ repeated one and a third million times. The library contains all of these, because they are possible.

But the library also contains every meaningful book. Every novel ever written. Every novel that could ever be written. Every history textbook, true and false. Every scientific paper, including the ones that have not yet been written. Every love letter you might send, and every love letter you might not. The book describing what you will do tomorrow, in correct detail. The book describing what you will do tomorrow, in incorrect detail. Every page of the catalogue that says where the true books are, and every page of the one that says so wrongly. Anything longer than one volume is there too, in installments, each volume shelved apart from the rest.

The library contains everything that can be written.

\section*{Generating the library}

Pause on how cheap this is to specify.

You do not need to describe the library book by book. You need to describe a procedure: \emph{produce every possible string of 1,312,000 characters from a 25-symbol alphabet, and bind each one as a book}. Three lines of pseudocode. The entire library, in all its unfathomable size, is the output of a tiny program.

The procedure does not know what is in the books. It does not care whether a string is gibberish or genius. It produces all of them, indiscriminately. The library's totality takes almost no information to specify. Its contents are inexhaustible.

This is the first hint. The thing that feels like the hard problem (the universe of all possible books) is not the hard problem. The thing that feels like the small problem (this book, the one you wanted) is the hard one.

\section*{Indexing}

Generation is trivial.

Indexing is everything.

That word, indexing, is the first name this book gives the cut: the act of picking your place out of everything that could be. By the end of the chapter it will have a starker one.

In Borges' library, finding any specific book is the entire challenge. Not because the library is hidden. The library is, by hypothesis, fully laid out. Every book is on a shelf, at a specific gallery, at a specific position. The library is complete and accessible. What is missing is a way to point to the book you want.

A catalogue would help. But the catalogue is also made of books, shelved somewhere in the library. So is the catalogue of catalogues. So is the catalogue that lies about the locations of the true catalogues. You cannot find the catalogue without already knowing where it is. The library contains the answer to every question, and the address of every answer, and the lie about the address of every answer. It contains the catalogue that lists only true books, the catalogue that looks like that catalogue but is wrong in one position, the catalogue that is wrong in two, and so on through every degree of corruption. Without a starting handle, you can search for centuries without retrieving anything but noise.

What you have, when you stand in front of a wall of identical-looking volumes, is a problem of \emph{indexing}: a way of picking out, from the totality of what could be written, the part that is relevant to where you are. The library is the space of possibility. An index is a pointer into it, and you have to build your own.

The naive picture would have it that the universe of possibilities is the mystery and your local situation is the small concrete fact. The library says the opposite. The universe of possibilities is the cheap thing. The local fact is the expensive thing. Information is in the cut.

\section*{The address of a book}

Here is how expensive, in Borges' own currency.

Suppose the librarians had numbered the shelves, so that every book has an address, and suppose the address is written in the library's own alphabet of 25 symbols. There are $25^{1{,}312{,}000}$ books to tell apart. An address of $k$ symbols can tell apart $25^k$ things, so the addresses need $1{,}312{,}000$ symbols each: 410 pages of 40 lines of 80 characters. The address of a book is a book.\footnote{Borges' narrator hopes for a catalogue of catalogues, and for the Man of the Book, who has read the one volume that is the compendium of all the rest. The arithmetic is less kind than the story. A faithful catalogue of the true books would run to as many volumes as there are true books, each volume shelved somewhere in the library, each as lost as the book it names.}

That settles the catalogue. One volume has room for exactly one address. A catalogue of a thousand true books is at least a thousand volumes, and a catalogue of the whole library is the whole library over again, with every entry as hard to find as the book it points to. Looking for the catalogue is the same problem as looking for the book. And there is one thing no catalogue could carry, however faithful, and however small: a mark that says which reader is holding this copy. The mark would be right for one reader and wrong for every other, and nothing in the library says which reader that is. Chapter~9 returns to that.

The numbers are not an accident of Borges' design. To name one of $N$ things with a fixed-length name takes about $\log N$ symbols, the logarithm taken in the size of the alphabet, and the library was built so that $N$ is the number of possible books, which makes $\log N$ one book. The result is general. Any complete library of fixed-size volumes is exactly as large as its own index.

Fixed-length addresses are the worst case, and for some books you can do better. The book that is the letter $e$ repeated, one and a third million times, was named a few pages ago in a dozen words. A name like that is a short description, and a short description is a cut made cheaply. How many books have one? Count. In any scheme of description, however clever, the descriptions are themselves strings, and each description names at most one book, and there are fewer short strings than long ones. In the binary alphabet, fewer than $2^{n-k}$ strings are shorter than $n-k$ symbols. So of the $2^n$ strings of length $n$, fewer than $2^{n-k}$ can have a description more than $k$ symbols shorter than themselves, because there are not enough short descriptions to go around. Fewer than one string in a thousand can be shortened by more than ten symbols; fewer than one in a million, by more than twenty. In Borges' alphabet, fewer than one book in $25^k$ has a name more than $k$ symbols shorter than itself. This is counting, and it holds for every scheme of description at once. Schemes disagree about \emph{which} books get the short names; for the universal machines of Chapter~4, Chapter~5 says how far they can disagree. No scheme can make short names common.

So almost every book in the library costs as much to name as to write out. The cheap cuts are rare, and they are the books with a pattern in them: the book of one letter, the book that repeats a page, the book of consequences drawn from a short rule. A law is a short name for a long book. Here is one. Imagine the book that records, night by night and to the eye, where the Moon stood among the stars on every night of the last thousand years. It is a long book, and by the count above there is no reason to expect it to have a short name. It has one anyway. Newton's law of gravitation, a few symbols, together with where the Sun, the Earth, and the Moon were on one night and how fast each was moving, generates the whole book, night after night, for those thousand years and a good many more. The book is enormous; its name fits on a page. That compression is what it means for the Moon's motion to be lawful, and it is most of why the law was worth finding. That is what \emph{indexing is everything} comes to when you count. By the measure of what it costs to say, the library is nearly all noise, and the few things in it that can be said cheaply are the only things anyone could ever find.

\section*{From books to programs}

The same trick generalizes far beyond books.

Borges fixed his books at 410 pages.\footnote{He fixed the alphabet too: twenty-two letters, the comma, the period, and the space. His shelves hold no digits, so none of this book's formulas is on them. This book is on the next shelves, not his.} Let go of that constraint. Consider every possible computer program, of any length, in any reasonable programming language. The space of all such programs is, again, trivial to specify. Programs are finite strings of symbols. You can enumerate them in order of length: one-symbol programs, then two-symbol programs, then three-symbol programs, and so on. The procedure is a few lines. The list goes on forever.\footnote{This kind of enumeration is a central object of computability theory. In 1936, Alan Turing showed that a single universal machine can run any program you give it; run it on all programs in interleaved steps and it produces everything computable. The machine itself is a small object. What it can compute is everything any Turing machine can. That every effective procedure is of this kind is the Church-Turing thesis: a thesis, not a theorem. Chapter~6 returns to it.} Figure~\ref{fig:enumeration-tree} shows the structure: every binary string of length $n$ is a leaf at depth $n$ in a binary tree.

Most strings of symbols are not valid programs at all. Of the valid ones, many run forever without producing anything. Some, when run, produce coherent output: numbers, sequences, images, behaviors. Fewer produce output that looks like an actual physical process; fewer still look like the universe you observe.

All of these programs are already in the enumeration. The enumeration is one rule. Running them produces every computable sequence: every chess game, every fictional history, every weather pattern a model could print. The program that draws a galaxy. The program that simulates a galaxy. The program that simulates a universe with slightly different physical constants. If your universe is computably describable (Chapter~6 does not take that for granted), then somewhere in the list is the program that produces your visual cortex's response to morning light, and the program that produces a universe identical to yours, except for one molecule. The program that has read a trillion words of human writing and predicts the next one. The program that produces this book.

The library of books is small. The library of programs is the larger structure of which the library of books is one tiny shelf.

The space of all programs is cheap. Locating the specific program whose output matches your data is the hard problem. ``Where am I in the library of programs'' has the same shape as ``where am I in Borges' library.'' The library is larger; the question is the same.

\section*{A note on the metaphor}

The library of books, the library of programs, the enumeration tree: each decomposes the totality into discrete items, neatly numbered. The decomposition is convenient. The rest of this book often speaks this way. But the underlying structure does not require it. Any regularity in the bit-sequences is exploitable by some program; programs can share substructure across what we call separate items. The decomposition into ``books'' or ``programs'' is a tool the cartographer brings; whether the territory has a decomposition of its own is a question Chapter~6 takes up.

When this book uses \emph{index} or \emph{book} or \emph{coordinate}, the discreteness is a working approximation, a label put on the structure. Chapter~14 returns to this seriously, in parallel with the dissolution of the self.

\section*{Empty and universal}

There is a way to make the cheapness of the totality sharper still.

Consider the empty set: the collection that contains nothing. You can describe it in one symbol: $\emptyset$. Now consider the universal set: the collection that contains everything. You can describe it in one phrase: \emph{everything}.

Both are easy to write down. Both are short to specify. The empty set is cheap because there is nothing to enumerate. The universal set is cheap because the enumeration is too long to perform, and you sidestep it by referring to the totality. Either extreme is, informationally, almost free.

The expensive descriptions are the ones in the middle. The set of all books that are coherent. The set of all programs whose output matches your observations. The set of all configurations of matter that constitute the inside of your living room. Each of these is a subset of the universal set, and each one requires substantial information to specify, because each one is a particular cut.

A theory that explains \emph{everything} explains nothing. A theory that explains \emph{nothing} explains nothing. A theory that explains \emph{this} is the only interesting thing. The act of explanation is the act of indexing.

This is the inversion the rest of the book is built on. There are no joints. There are only the cuts you make. Every act of explanation, every located fact, every theory that picks out \emph{this} one thing, is a cut. You will meet the cut three times in what follows: as the index you make to predict, as the system you call the world, and as the self that does the cutting. They are one move, met at three depths.

Part of the inversion is already fact: no algorithm takes a string and returns its complexity or its shortest description, so no computable procedure sorts the Library by how cheaply each thing in it can be said. Nor can you tell by looking. Take as many digits of $\pi$ as you like: no statistical test run on them has found a pattern, and they have a one-line name, a short program that prints the digits in order for as long as you ask. To call a thing in the Library noise is to report only that no short name for it is known. Whether it has one is a separate fact. Counting says it rarely does, and a short name, once found, is its own certificate; but no procedure can certify, for every string, that it has none. Call that the one-way certificate, because the rest of the book will use it by name: a found name certifies itself, and an absence cannot be certified.

A bare address, like the path in Figure~\ref{fig:enumeration-tree}, is computable, but it is only the string again; any index that tracks what matters to you is one you brought. The stronger reading, that the totality has no correct decomposition at all and the books, the programs, the tidy tree of strings are carvings brought to a structure with none of its own, is not a theorem; Chapter~6 will ask you to take it as a commitment, and Chapter~7 will show you what refusing it costs.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

Step back.

You are an observer with data. Your data is, like the chicken's, a small finite slice of a vastly larger structure. The structure is, like Borges' library, cheap to specify in its totality and expensive to navigate. Given your data, what is the right way to point to where you are in the structure?

This is the question. You have been answering it your whole life, under another name.

It is called probability.
