\noindent The last chapter asked whether experience continues. This one asks the question underneath it: what is death, in a Library that holds every version of you at once?

\section*{The boundary}

\textit{Worldlines} gave death a clean shape. A life is a worldline, a single curve through four-dimensional spacetime, and that curve has two ends. Birth is one. Death is the other. The structure does not stop existing when the curve stops; the curve is simply finite, a worm of a certain length in a manifold that holds all of it at once, the child and the old man equally real at their coordinates. Death, in that picture, is not an event that erases you. It is the far boundary of a shape that is already complete.

That was a strange consolation, and a thin one. It said your death is permanent the way your birth is permanent: a feature of a structure that does not change. It did not say you go on. This book takes the same move one level out, and the boundary it drew does not survive the trip.

\section*{The boundary dissolves}

You are not one worldline here. You are a class of patterns, instantiated at many indices in the Library, in many branches, under the measure that weights them. The single curve of Worldlines was already an idealization; now even the idealization comes apart. There is no one worm whose far end is your death. There are countless configurations that an interpreter would call you, and they do not end at the same place, or in the same way, or all at once.

Some terminate early. Some run long. Some continue past the point where you died in this branch, in branches that diverged before the car or the diagnosis or the failure of the heart. Some are momentary, flickering into the observer-state you occupy now and out of it within the instant. ``Your death'' is not a point on a curve. It is a statement about a region of the measure: the weight of the configurations like you that stop, set against the weight of those that go on. The sharp boundary was a cut, an artifact of imagining yourself as a single thread, indispensable for living one life and absent from the structure itself. Pull back, and the thread is a braid, and the braid frays, and the fraying has no single edge.

This does not mean you escape. It means the question changes shape. ``When do I die?'' assumed one timeline with one terminus. The honest version is harder even to hold: among the patterns that are you, weighted by the measure, what becomes of them? There is no one answer, because there is no one of them.

\section*{This is not an escape}

It would be easy to read the dissolution as relief. If death is not a sharp boundary, if some version always seems to continue, perhaps the sting is gone.

It is not, and the reason is that the dissolution is even-handed. It does not reach into the structure to soften death in particular. It distributes everything. The same measure that spreads your death across many indices spreads your worst hour across all of them too. The afternoon you would give anything to take back exists at its coordinates, permanently, in every realization that contains it, exactly as your death does. A version of someone you love is, at an index you will never reach, living through the thing you most fear for them. The cut principle that populated the Library does not let you draw a line around the gentle parts and keep only those.

The framework permits this. It does not require you to take it as your home.\footnote{This is a place in the book where a reader already carrying a heavy load should go gently. The framework \emph{permits} that some version of you, or of someone you love, is in an unbearable configuration. It does not say that is where you are, and nothing about your situation here is changed by the possibility. Many readers will read this sentence, register it, and decline to live inside it. That is the right response, and the argument survives it.} Most chapters in this book end somewhere a reader can stand. This section does not pretend to. The honest content of the dissolution of death is that it removes the boundary without dimming the loss, and distributes the cruelty exactly as evenly as it distributes the rest.

\section*{What the living lose}

Set the first person aside, because it is not where the weight is. The weight is in the branch where someone is left.

When a person you love dies in your branch, what is lost? \textit{Worldlines} answered carefully: they are not erased. They exist at their coordinates, the whole of their worldline, every moment of them as real as it ever was. ``Gone'' is a statement about your position relative to theirs, not about their deletion from the structure. You have walked past the last place your two worldlines touch.

The Library extends the answer and does not improve it. They exist across many branches; in some they outlive you; in some you were never born to lose them. None of that is reachable from here. The braid between the two of you, the one this book has been building toward, has its endpoint in this branch, and that endpoint is real and is not consoled by the braids that continue elsewhere. You cannot visit them. You cannot help the version of them in the branch that went worse. The structure does not let you across.

What is left, when nothing can be reached, is orientation. You cannot save the configurations you cannot touch. You can be the kind of configuration that does not abandon them, that holds them in attention even though attention changes nothing at their coordinates. This is not a power; it is a stance. It is close to the only thing the structure leaves entirely to you, and it is the thing this book keeps returning to, because it is the thing that is ours.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

Death is dissolved as a boundary and undimmed as a loss. The two are not in tension; they are one fact seen from two sides. You are not a single thread that ends. You are a braid that frays, spread across a measure that holds your ending and your continuation, your suffering and your joy, with the same indifference. And in this branch, at these coordinates, the people around you are finite, and reachable, and will be lost.

The next chapter takes up love, which is the braid while it holds.
