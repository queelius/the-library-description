\chapter{Anthropic Assumptions}\label{app:anthropics}

Chapter 7 named a gap, the missing occurrence measure, and Chapter 9 named the principle behind it (no description contains its center) and the three things any argument in its family needs: a supplier of occurrences, a rule for \emph{now}, and a rule for \emph{next}. This appendix surveys the rules offered for the two faces. $M$ is a semimeasure over program-output prefixes, not a probability distribution over observer occurrences. To ask which matching occurrence is \emph{you}, one must first define a reference class or sample space and then assign self-location weights. Cosmological claims about ordinary observers and fluctuations require still more: a physical model that produces the occurrences. This appendix sketches several additions and explains why the book adopts none.

The \emph{Self-Sampling Assumption} (SSA) says: reason as if your current observer-moment were sampled from a specified reference class, for instance observers with similar information or members of a species. Once the class and its weighting are fixed, SSA can update beliefs about your place within it. This is one route to the doomsday argument. Its answer depends on who enters the class and what ``sampled'' means.

The \emph{Self-Indication Assumption} (SIA) gives hypotheses more weight when they contain more eligible observers like you. It thereby couples existence evidence to population size, once eligibility and counting have been defined.

The assumptions can disagree on Sleeping Beauty, the doomsday argument, and fluctuation observers. SSA is sensitive to the reference class; SIA faces the ``presumptuous philosopher'' problem. A more general approach specifies a \emph{xerographic distribution}, an explicit first-person probability over otherwise matching occurrences. That notation makes the missing choice visible rather than pretending $M$ already made it. None follows from Bayes, Kraft, $M$, or dominance.

The \emph{speed prior}, due to Schmidhuber, changes the program prior by penalizing computation time as well as description length. It can downweight outputs that require long computations, at the cost of disfavoring computational depth. But it still weights programs or outputs. It does not by itself identify observer occurrences, define a reference class, or provide a complete solution to the Boltzmann-observer problem.

One proposal builds the rule for \emph{now} out of the machinery of Part I, and it deserves its own paragraph. UDASSA, the universal distribution joined to an absolute self-selection assumption, proposed by Wei Dai and set out by Hal Finney, weights an observer-moment by its weight under the universal distribution, which the coding theorem of Appendix~\ref{app:bedrock} makes, up to a constant, the length of the shortest program that generates its world \emph{and} locates the observer-moment inside it; the universal distribution over strings becomes a distribution over observer-moments. It is the most principled candidate for an occurrence measure anyone has offered, and the book's reply is not that it is wrong. The locating program is an index; its length is relative to a machine, with the invariance constant Chapter 5 said is not small; and the decision to weight by that length is the choice of a rule. Used for a job, with its scope stated, it is as legitimate as $M$ itself, which the book also uses for a job and crowns nowhere. What it cannot do is what the gap asked for: find the center in the description. It draws one, well.

The proposals do not all do the same job. SSA, SIA, xerographic distributions, and UDASSA add indexical structure. The speed prior changes program weights. A cosmological model determines which physical occurrences are present. Combining them can produce a sharper answer, but each ingredient must be defended separately.

\section*{Quantum immortality}

Chapter 11 names an argument, usually called quantum immortality, that combines a physical interpretation with additions of the kind this appendix describes. Its best-known form is Max Tegmark's 1998 ``quantum suicide'' thought experiment; earlier versions are listed in the Notes to Chapter 11. Its premises can be taken one at a time.

The first is a supplier of occurrences. Everett's reading of quantum mechanics, taken ontically, is the usual one: a process that may kill an observer yields decoherent branches, some with a surviving observer and some without. Chapter 8 treats that reading as a landmark, not as evidence. A spatially infinite cosmos does the same work with no interpretation of quantum mechanics at all, as Chapter 9 showed, and so does any other supplier of matching occurrences; the argument's form does not change.

The second is an identity rule, a criterion saying which later observer-moments continue a given person. Without one, nothing settles which survivors count as the same person.

The third is a self-location rule that assigns first-person weight only among observer-moments that can report an outcome, renormalizing over them. This is a reference-class choice of exactly the kind SSA requires.

The argument then rests on a selection effect that is true and nearly trivial: no one reports from a history without a reporter. Every report of survival is made by a survivor.

The selection effect does not deliver the conclusion. It restricts who can report. It does not define a single first-person sequence through the branches, and it does not show that the surviving branches keep substantial weight. Quantum prediction uses Born weights, and in a repeated lethal experiment the surviving branches carry a share that shrinks with each trial (a half, then a quarter, and so on, in Tegmark's setup). The argument reaches ``you will experience survival'' only by setting those weights aside in favor of the renormalized self-location rule, and that step is the one in dispute. Even with all three premises granted, the conclusion would concern which reports exist, not how the reporter fares. Chapter 9 catalogued a darker variant; it needs the same premises, and this book adopts none of them. And, as Chapter 9 showed with the infinite cosmos as the case, the copies were already there; ``you die and then continue as the survivor'' is the identity rule speaking.

Nothing in the argument is a reason to take any real risk. If the subject has stopped being abstract for you, turn to \hyperref[resources]{\textit{Resources}, at the back of the book}.

\section*{What the book adopts}

This book adopts none, not as a verdict against them but as a matter of discipline. A reader who wants a sharper answer must specify an ontology, a cosmology where relevant, an occurrence definition, and a self-location distribution rather than hiding those choices inside the phrase ``universal prior.''
