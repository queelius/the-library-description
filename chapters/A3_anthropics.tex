\chapter{Anthropic Assumptions}\label{app:anthropics}

Chapter 8 left a question standing on purpose: among the observers in the multiverse who match your current data, your memories, your sensory state, this moment of reading, which one is \emph{you}? Chapter 9 named a second question the framework permits but does not close: whether the measure over observers should be adjusted to weigh down the stranger regions it allows, the momentary fluctuations and the vast lawless majority. Both questions have generated proposals in the literature. Three recur often enough to state here plainly, together with the reason this book adopts none of them.

The \emph{Self-Sampling Assumption} (SSA) says: reason as if you are a random sample drawn from some reference class of observers, for instance all observers with your current information state, or all observers in your species' history. Once a reference class is fixed, SSA lets you say something about your place within it: if most members of the class are not absurdly early in a long-lived history, then finding yourself early is evidence against that history being long. This is the reasoning behind the doomsday argument. SSA would answer part of \emph{where am I} by fiat: name a class, weight uniformly across it, and read off a posterior.

The \emph{Self-Indication Assumption} (SIA) says: weight a hypothesis about the world by how many observers like you it contains, so that a hypothesis predicting a large population of matching observers gains credence for that reason alone. SIA would add an answer to a different question, why the universe (or the Library) should be expected to favor populous regions: because, conditional on existing at all, you are more likely to exist somewhere with many of you than somewhere with few.

The two assumptions disagree with each other on the Sleeping Beauty problem, on the doomsday argument, and on how much weight to give Boltzmann brains, and each carries its own known difficulty: SSA's answer depends on an arbitrary choice of reference class; SIA implies, notoriously, that a hypothesis favoring more observers should always be preferred absent contrary evidence, the ``presumptuous philosopher'' problem. Neither is a theorem. Neither follows from Bayes, Kraft, the universal prior, or dominance.

The \emph{speed prior}, due to Schmidhuber, penalizes not only the length of a description but the running time of the program that produces it, so that two programs producing the same output are weighted apart by how long each takes to run. Chapter 9 already noted, in passing, what this would buy: a Boltzmann brain is reached by an astronomically long computation, a vast thermodynamic fluctuation, while an embedded observer with a genuine lawful history is reached comparatively fast, so a runtime penalty suppresses the former relative to the universal prior's silence on the matter. It also costs something the same footnote named: it disfavors computationally deep structures generally, and a rich, long-evolved universe is presumably one of these.

What the three share is the same shape. Each is a further cut laid on top of the postulate that no cut is privileged: a reference class, a population weighting, a runtime penalty. None is demanded by the bedrock, and none is demanded by the postulate itself, which asks only that no cut be treated as the correct one, not that the reader supply one of these three and treat \emph{it} as correct instead. Each buys a sharper answer, to \emph{where am I} or to the Boltzmann brain problem, at the price of an additional, undefended carving.

This book adopts none of them, not as a verdict against them but as a matter of discipline: the postulate does the work it can do, and the rest is left open. A reader who wants a sharper answer than this book gives is free to adopt SSA, SIA, or the speed prior and see what changes; the bedrock survives the choice, the way it survives declining the postulate itself. This appendix maps that further territory. It does not recommend entering it.
