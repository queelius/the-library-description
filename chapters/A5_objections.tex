\chapter{Objections and Replies}\label{app:objections}

The strongest objections deserve the strongest form of themselves before any reply. What follows states seven of them at full strength, then answers while keeping the five footings distinct: theorem, construction, finitude, postulate, and optional additions. If an objection cannot be met within those limits, it should stand.

\emph{This is anti-realism, or instrumentalism, in disguise.} The book keeps saying that no cut is privileged, that realism is a cut, that even the self is a cut. Strip the vocabulary and this is the old idealist move: nothing is really out there; it is all observer-relative construction.

The postulate declines to privilege one decomposition as uniquely correct. The book is realist about neither the outside view nor the inside view by fiat: program space is a mathematical object, but whether every described output is physically or metaphysically real remains open. Chapter 8 says why: choosing which picture is the real one is itself a cut, and the postulate privileges none. Local experiences and effects do not vanish because their boundaries are modeled. This is not straightforward anti-realism, because the book does not assert that nothing mind-independent exists. It is also not emphatic realism about the totality. It is disciplined agnosticism about what the formal construction licenses ontologically.

\emph{The universal prior is uncomputable, so it is idle.} $M$ cannot be computed; $K(x)$ cannot be computed. A quantity no agent can ever calculate is a curiosity, not a foundation, and a worldview built on it is built on sand.

Uncomputability does not make $M$ mathematically idle. Dominance and convergence make it a theoretical benchmark. But those theorems do not imply that every practical predictor approximates $M$, or that a computable method has a useful finite error bound merely because $M$ exists. Finitude explains why real agents use bounded local models; it does not make those models targets of one unique prior. The objection therefore limits the engineering claim while leaving the theoretical results intact.

\emph{Simplicity is smuggled in as a value.} The book leans on simplicity everywhere: shorter is more probable, the simple system is the one you find yourself in. But ``prefer the simple'' is an aesthetic taste, quietly installed as though it were mathematics.

Part of the objection is correct. Kraft's inequality makes the fair-bit weights $2^{-|p|}$ admissible; it does not compel an arbitrary prior to use them or put most mass on short programs. The construction chooses fair random bits and a universal machine. Its justification is then cumulative: universality, dominance, convergence, and the practical usefulness of compression. Machine choice remains important at finite scales. Simplicity is not merely taste, but neither is it smuggled out of Kraft as a unique value-free command. And the book never says the simple system is the one you find yourself in. $M$ weights programs, not observer occurrences; a claim about where you find yourself would need the missing occurrence measure of Chapter 7, which the book does not supply.

\emph{The turn to care is a non-sequitur from the math.} Fifteen chapters of cold structure, and then, with no valid inference, the book turns to kindness and closes on ``Stay.'' Nothing in Bayes, Kraft, or Solomonoff entails that you should care about anyone. The ending is a sentimental addition, the very consolation the book claims to refuse.

This objection is correct, and the book says so in its own text: ``The structure does not require it. The structure does not reward it. The structure does not notice.'' Care is not derived from the structure. The mathematics is silent on what to do. The turn is not an inference. It is a choice, a cut made without the structure's permission, because no permission was ever coming. The book never pretends otherwise. It calls the ending not an argument, and it asks outright, ``Is it enough? I do not know.'' A book that claimed the mathematics entailed kindness would be consoling you with something untrue, and that is the one move this book refuses. Care is held anyway, with no floor under it that the book can certify, and that is stated plainly rather than hidden. The objection names the gap. The book was already standing in it, on purpose.

\emph{The multiverse is unfalsifiable, so this is not science.} The book posits every computable world, hellscapes and Boltzmann observers among them. None of it can be tested. By the usual criterion that is not science but metaphysics in a lab coat.

The book agrees that the outside view, read ontically, is not established as a physical hypothesis. It does not assert that the multiverse exists, and it does not borrow confirmation from quantum experiments. The theorem-level claims are mathematical; applications to prediction can be tested; the postulate is philosophical; Everett and any all-realities ontology are interpretations. Falsifiability is the right demand for empirical claims, which is precisely why the book does not present the optional ontology as confirmed science.

\emph{Some cuts really do carve at the joints.} Nature has a grain. Chemistry found the elements and not someone's favorite lumps of matter; physics found mass and charge and not gerrymandered mixtures of them; and the whole success of science is that some carvings track structure that was there before anyone carved. The book's own Chapter 8 concedes it: inference works only where the world is hierarchically decomposable, which is to say only where there are joints to find. ``No cut is privileged'' is refuted by every theory that works.

The objection runs together two senses of \emph{privileged}, and the book grants the first. Some carvings work, for a job, and the structure is not indifferent to which: a cut that isolates a decomposable subsystem predicts, and one that straddles the coupling does not, and Chapter 8 says why without embarrassment. Part I made the point exact. On any machine, almost every string has no short description (Chapter 2's count), so that a few strings do is a fact about the strings relative to that machine, not a taste. Two things stop that fact from crowning a carving. First, which strings get the short names depends on the machine, and invariance bounds the disagreement by a constant it does not make small; the joint, if it is one, sits at the asymptote, where no finite observer stands; and, by the analogy Chapter 6 drew from Chapter 2's one-way certificate, for the other direction: neither a joint nor its absence can be certified from inside, which is why the postulate declines rather than denies. Second, \emph{works} is always \emph{works for a job}, and the jobs are many. The kitchen of Chapter 13, reread in Chapter 14, is carved at the grain of mugs and at the grain of molecules; the chooser of Chapter 11 is carved at five widths, from a few hundred milliseconds to a culture; and each carving works for the question that asked for it. Being rewarded is not being the structure's own. What the postulate declines is only the second sense: that among the carvings that work there is one the structure has independently of every job. A reader who wants exactly that is refusing the postulate, which Chapter 7 and Appendix~\ref{app:refusing} allow, and keeps every theorem.

\emph{The occurrence measure is not missing; the book's own prior supplies it.} Chapter 9 says nothing in the mathematics weights observer occurrences. But the universal distribution does, once you let it: weight an observer-moment by the length of the shortest program that generates its world and locates the moment within it. That is UDASSA, and it is built from exactly the machinery of Part I, with nothing added that the book has not already accepted. The gap the book keeps calling missing has been filled, by the book's own prior, and the refusal to use it is a choice the book has dressed up as a limit.

Half of this is granted in Appendix~\ref{app:anthropics}: UDASSA is the most principled candidate anyone has offered, and used for a job it is as legitimate as $M$. The objection fails at the word \emph{supplies}. Three choices sit inside the proposal, and none is in the mathematics. The locating program is an index, a cut that picks one occurrence out of a world, and Chapter 2 counted what that costs. Its length is relative to a machine, and invariance bounds the disagreement by a constant it does not make small, so two users of UDASSA with different machines weight the same occurrence differently and nothing in the construction says which of them is right. And the decision to weight occurrences by locating length at all is a rule, adopted; weights come after cuts, and this is the cut. Why is that disqualifying for UDASSA and not for $M$, since $M$ is machine-relative too? It is not disqualifying for either. The book uses $M$ for a job, prediction, with its scope stated in Chapter 5, and crowns it nowhere; a reader may use UDASSA the same way, for a job, with its scope stated. What the objection asked for was more than a job. It asked for the occurrence that is you, found in the description rather than drawn on it, and no description contains its center. The book declines that crown for the same reason it declines every other. The measure is not missing because no one has proposed one. It is missing because every proposal is a cut.
