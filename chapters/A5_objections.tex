\chapter{Objections and Replies}\label{app:objections}

The strongest objections deserve the strongest form of themselves before any reply. What follows states five of them at full strength, then answers while keeping theorem, construction, postulate, and practical limit distinct. If an objection cannot be met within those limits, it should stand.

\emph{This is anti-realism, or instrumentalism, in disguise.} The book keeps saying that no cut is privileged, that realism is a cut, that even the self is a cut. Strip the vocabulary and this is the old idealist move: nothing is really out there; it is all observer-relative construction.

The postulate declines to privilege one decomposition as uniquely correct. The book is realist about neither Picture A nor Picture B by fiat: program space is a mathematical object, but whether every described output is physically or metaphysically real remains open. Local experiences and effects do not vanish because their boundaries are modeled. This is not straightforward anti-realism, because the book does not assert that nothing mind-independent exists. It is also not emphatic realism about the totality. It is disciplined agnosticism about what the formal construction licenses ontologically.

\emph{The universal prior is uncomputable, so it is idle.} $M$ cannot be computed; $K(x)$ cannot be computed. A quantity no agent can ever calculate is a curiosity, not a foundation, and a worldview built on it is built on sand.

Uncomputability does not make $M$ mathematically idle. Dominance and convergence make it a theoretical benchmark. But those theorems do not imply that every practical predictor approximates $M$, or that a computable method has a useful finite error bound merely because $M$ exists. Finitude explains why real agents use bounded local models; it does not make those models targets of one unique prior. The objection therefore limits the engineering claim while leaving the theoretical results intact.

\emph{Simplicity is smuggled in as a value.} The book leans on simplicity everywhere: shorter is more probable, the simple system is the one you find yourself in. But ``prefer the simple'' is an aesthetic taste, quietly installed as though it were mathematics.

Part of the objection is correct. Kraft's inequality makes the fair-bit weights $2^{-|p|}$ admissible; it does not compel an arbitrary prior to use them or put most mass on short programs. The construction chooses fair random bits and a universal machine. Its justification is then cumulative: universality, dominance, convergence, and the practical usefulness of compression. Machine choice remains important at finite scales. Simplicity is not merely taste, but neither is it smuggled out of Kraft as a unique value-free command.

\emph{The turn to care is a non-sequitur from the math.} Fifteen chapters of cold structure, and then, with no valid inference, the book turns to kindness and closes on ``Stay.'' Nothing in Bayes, Kraft, or Solomonoff entails that you should care about anyone. The ending is a sentimental addition, the very consolation the book claims to refuse.

This objection is correct, and the book says so in its own text. Care is not derived from the structure. The mathematics is silent on what to do; the structure does not require care, does not reward it, does not notice it. The turn is not an inference. It is a choice, a cut made without the structure's permission, because no permission was ever coming. The book never pretends otherwise. It calls the ending not an argument, and it asks outright, ``Is it enough? I do not know.'' The honesty is the point, not a defect to be patched. A book that claimed the mathematics entailed kindness would be consoling you with something untrue, and that is the one move this book refuses. Care is held anyway, with no floor under it, and that there is no floor is stated plainly rather than hidden. The objection names the gap. The book was already standing in it, on purpose.

\emph{The multiverse is unfalsifiable, so this is not science.} The book posits every computable world, hellscapes and Boltzmann observers among them. None of it can be tested. By the usual criterion that is not science but metaphysics in a lab coat.

The book agrees that Picture A is not established as a physical hypothesis. It does not assert that the multiverse exists, and it does not borrow confirmation from quantum experiments. The theorem-level claims are mathematical; applications to prediction can be tested; the postulate is philosophical; Everett and any all-realities ontology are interpretations. Falsifiability is the right demand for empirical claims, which is precisely why the book does not present the optional ontology as confirmed science.
