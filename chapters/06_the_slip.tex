\noindent \textsc{Chapter 5 ended with a small phrase.} \emph{What remains is interpretation.} This chapter takes the first step the mathematics does not force.

So far, the universal prior $M$ has been a distribution on hypotheses. A way of weighting candidate explanations of the data you have. A prior over the theories you might hold about the world.

The step is to read $M$ differently. Not as a measure over what you might believe. As a measure over what is.

\section*{Two readings}

The equation $M(x) = \sum_{p\,:\,U(p) = x*} 2^{-|p|}$ does not say what $M(x)$ \emph{means}. It says how to compute it. Two interpretations are available, and they are not the same thing.

The first reading is epistemic. $M(x)$ is the probability that a careful reasoner should assign to observing $x$, given no prior data. The universal prior describes optimal belief. It tells the agent how to weight her hypotheses about the world. Nothing in this reading commits to anything about the world itself; the universal prior is a fact about reasoning, not about reality.

The second reading is ontic. $M(x)$ is the measure of \emph{realities} that produce $x$. The universal prior describes the frequency with which $x$ appears among the things that exist. Under this reading, $M$ is a fact about reality, not about reasoning.

The mathematics is the same. The metaphysical commitment is different.

\section*{The one move: no cut is privileged}

To read $M$ as a measure over what is, rather than over what you should believe, takes one step. Naming it precisely is the work of this chapter, because once it is named you can see how small it is, and you can refuse it.

Start with the language. To speak of reality in terms of $M$ at all is to describe the world as a computable structure, a thing some program produces. This is the physical reading of the Church-Turing thesis: any process physically realizable can be computed by a Turing machine. It is not the original thesis (that one, about algorithms, has been settled and tested for ninety years without a counterexample); it is a claim about what physics is doing. The empirical case is strong. Every physical process ever measured falls within the Turing-computable class; every framework that has survived experimental test, from Newtonian mechanics through quantum field theory, fits inside it; the claim that physics does some genuinely uncomputable thing in a load-bearing way has no experimental support. But notice what this step is and is not. It fixes the \emph{language} in which descriptions are written, the medium in which we cut. It is not yet a claim about which descriptions are real. Computability is the frame within which we cut, not a privileged fact about a world standing behind the descriptions.

Now the step itself. The naive reading says there is one world, and $M$ tells you how to reason about it. But ``there is one world, and it is this one'' is not the cheap default it feels like. It is a cut, and, as the next section shows, the single most expensive thing you could specify, because you must say \emph{which} world is the real one. The step this chapter takes is to decline that cut. Not to add worlds: to stop privileging one.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The postulate: no cut is privileged.} The totality of computable structures has no decomposition that is the correct one, and no element that is the one that is real. Every carving, including the carving that says only this world exists, is a cut an observer makes, and the structure ranks none above the others.
\end{quote}

This is the book's one interpretive commitment, and it is a refusal, not an addition. The Library of Babel from Chapter 2 is not a metaphor; but the postulate does not announce that ``all computable structures exist'' as some new and lavish fact. It says the opposite in spirit: the reason you took only one of them to be real was a cut you were making without noticing, and the cut is not forced. What is left when you stop making it is the uncut totality.

Be careful how far this reaches. ``No cut is privileged'' is not ``every structure is equally, robustly real.'' The leap from declining to privilege one world to asserting that all of them exist in the full-blooded sense is \emph{itself} a cut, and the book does not make it either. Whether the totality ``exists'' is a question the book holds open; Chapter 8 shows why it has no view-independent answer. For now the commitment is only the refusal: no carving is the true one.

What the postulate buys. Multiplicity, first: many structures are consistent with any partial data, so your observations restrict you to a region of the Library, never to a point. And a cleaner account of the simplicity bias: you expect short descriptions not because the structure prefers them, but because you are a finite cutter, and a finite cutter is forced toward cheap cuts. Chapter 4 showed why; Kraft makes short descriptions scarce, so finite belief has to spend itself on them. The bias is yours, not the world's.

What the postulate costs. A picture far larger than ordinary realism. The version of you that turned left at the bakery this morning is a carving as good as the one that turned right, and the structure prefers neither. That is a great deal to take on. The surprise of the next section is that it is also, by the only measure the book trusts, the \emph{cheaper} thing to take on.

\section*{Information is in the cut}

The standard objection to an ontology this large is the one Occam's razor is usually summoned for: it multiplies entities. By declining to privilege one world, the postulate leaves countless realities un-excluded, inflating the ontology far beyond the single universe we observe.\footnote{In the literature the nearest named position is Tegmark's Mathematical Universe Hypothesis, restricted to the computable structures. The book arrives there not as a hypothesis to adopt but as what is left when the one-world cut is declined, so it needs no name of its own here.} Occam's razor, the usual simplicity heuristic, seems to forbid such inflation. \emph{Do not multiply entities beyond necessity.}

The objection rests on a confusion the Library makes visible.

Chapter 2 established that informational cost lives in the cut, not in the totality. The empty set is cheap. The universal set is cheap. Both are describable in a phrase. The interesting subsets, the ones that require selecting some elements from a larger possibility space, are where description length lives.

Apply the principle to ontology. Two competing positions on what exists.

\emph{Position One: only this universe exists.} To assert this position, one must specify which universe is real. The specification is a long string. It must include the laws of dynamics, the values of every physical constant, the initial conditions of the universe, and the address of our universe within the Library of all possibilities. The information cost is the Kolmogorov complexity of our particular universe, which is large.

\emph{Position Two: no world is privileged.} To decline the first position, one specifies only the generator. The generator is a few lines of pseudocode: \emph{enumerate all programs of increasing length, and let each compute its output}.\footnote{``Generator'' is metaphor. The Library is not produced by an algorithm running in time; it is a static object, the set of all programs runnable on a universal Turing machine. The few lines of pseudocode are our way of indexing the Library, not its constitution. This mirrors the treatment of physical spacetime in \textit{Worldlines}: a static four-dimensional manifold that we traverse via our worldlines. The Library, like the manifold, is timeless. Our use of words like ``generation'' reflects how we encounter the Library, not what it is.} The information cost is the description length of the generator, which is small.\footnote{The generator's own description is a bit string of finite length, so it is itself one of the programs in the enumeration. The Library contains its own description. Under the postulate, the universe containing the algorithm that enumerates all universes is also in the Library. The self-reference is structural and harmless.}

By description length, the second position is the simpler ontology. The first specifies which universe is real; the second specifies nothing beyond the rule that generates the Library.

This does not prove the postulate. Applying a description-length principle to existence itself is a further step the bedrock does not force, and simplicity is one input among many to a metaphysical commitment. But the dialectic is reframed. The intuition that ``just one world'' is simpler is, by the cut principle, backwards. The single-universe view requires more information to specify, not less.

The book takes the postulate partly for this reason. It is the reading that requires the least cut.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

The slip has been made. The universal prior, which started as a way to weight hypotheses, is now a way to weight realities. The Library has been read ontologically.

The picture this gives, the older question it sharpens, and the limits of any of it are the subjects of the next chapter.
