\chapter{The Slip}

\chapterepigraph
{It from bit. Otherwise put, every `it' \ldots{} derives its function, its meaning, its very existence entirely \ldots{} from the apparatus-elicited answers to yes-or-no questions, binary choices, bits.}
{John Archibald Wheeler, ``Information, Physics, Quantum'' (1990)}

\noindent \textsc{Chapter 5 ended with a small phrase.} \emph{What remains is interpretation.} This chapter examines the first step the mathematics does not force.

So far, the universal semimeasure $M$ has weighted candidate programs whose outputs begin with the data you have. It is a prior for sequential prediction.

The temptation is to read $M$ differently. Not as a measure over candidate descriptions, but as a measure over what is.

\section*{Two readings}

The equation $M(x) = \sum_{p\,:\,U(p) = x{*}} 2^{-|p|}$ sums over the minimal programs $p$ whose output begins with $x$, as Chapter 4 built it. It does not say what $M(x)$ \emph{means}. It says how the weight is assembled. Two interpretations are available, and they are not the same thing.

The first reading is epistemic. $M(x)$ is the weight assigned to seeing a prefix $x$ before further data. The universal construction tells you how to weight candidate programs. Nothing in this reading commits to anything about the world itself.

The second reading is ontic. It proposes that programs describe realities and that $M(x)$ somehow measures the realities producing $x$. But that ``somehow'' contains extra work. The formula weights programs and output prefixes. It does not say which outputs exist, or why a weight on programs should become a weight on what is.

Notice what the second reading does. It draws a line around some outputs and calls what falls inside \emph{real}. That line is a cut, and nothing in the formula draws it.

The mathematics is the same. The metaphysical commitment is different. Figure~\ref{fig:slip} sets the two readings side by side.

This chapter exposes the second reading without quietly installing it. What the book does commit to is smaller, and it gets a name.

\section*{The one move: no cut is privileged}

Chapter 2 put its inversion in two sentences. \emph{There are no joints. There are only the cuts you make.} Part I earned a fact underneath them, and only a fact: complexity cannot be computed, so nothing computable sorts the Library by description cost. The sentences said more than the fact. They said that no decomposition at all, computable or not, is the correct one. Chapter 2's inversion is where this was first felt; here it becomes a commitment. A commitment should be named precisely, so that you can see its reach and refuse it.

Take it at the depth this Part is about. ``There is one world, decomposed in exactly this way'' is not a neutral description. It selects, from the space of computable descriptions,\footnote{That frame is a choice, not a finding. The original Church-Turing thesis concerns what counts as an effective procedure. The stronger \emph{physical} Church-Turing thesis, that every physically realizable process can be simulated by a Turing machine, is not a theorem, and physics has not settled it. The book works in computable descriptions because they are broad enough to express the theories it uses; the choice does not prove that nature is computable, and it does not say which descriptions are real.} a system and a boundary: these laws, these objects, this history. Part I cut the totality to predict. This cut gives you a world. The postulate declines to call it view-independently correct.

\begin{quote}
\textbf{The postulate: no cut is privileged.} No decomposition of the describable totality is view-independently correct. Every carving into worlds, systems, and selves is a cut an observer makes; none comes labeled as the structure's own.
\end{quote}

This is the book's one interpretive commitment, and it is a refusal, not an addition. It does not announce that all computable structures exist. Under Picture A, one may contemplate the totality of descriptions as an object. Under Picture B, a situated observer retains a posterior over candidate descriptions. The postulate does not choose which picture is real.

Be careful how far this reaches. ``No cut is privileged'' is not ``every structure is equally, robustly real.'' The leap from declining to privilege one world to asserting that all of them exist in the full-blooded sense is \emph{itself} a cut, and the book does not make it either. Whether the totality ``exists'' is a question the book holds open; Chapter 8 shows why it has no view-independent answer. For now the commitment is only the refusal: no carving is the true one.

What the postulate buys is underdetermination, held honestly. Many structures are consistent with partial data, and no decomposition arrives already crowned. Finitude is why you must cut anyway: a bounded observer needs local models. The theorems and the construction are why $M$ is a powerful ideal predictor over the cuts you might make. None of them makes simplicity the world's preference.

What the postulate costs is groundlessness. You still cut, and you must; but no cut comes with a floor under it, no view-independent answer to which decomposition is the real one. Picture A makes the cost vivid by contemplating many alternatives; Picture B keeps the uncertainty situated. The next section explains why the language of information tempts us toward Picture A, and why that temptation is not proof.

\section*{Information is in the cut}

A standard objection to Picture A is the one Occam's razor is usually summoned for: it multiplies entities. If every computable output is taken as a reality, the ontology is far larger than the single universe we observe.\footnote{A nearby position in the literature is Tegmark's Mathematical Universe Hypothesis, here restricted to computable structures. This book does not adopt it as a consequence of Solomonoff induction.} Occam's razor seems to forbid such inflation. \emph{Do not multiply entities beyond necessity.}

The objection mixes two kinds of cost, and the Library keeps them apart.

Chapter 2 established that informational cost lives in the cut, not in the totality. The empty set is cheap. The universal set is cheap. Both are describable in a phrase. The interesting subsets, the ones that require selecting some elements from a larger possibility space, are where description length lives.

Count entities, and Picture A is extravagant. Count description, and the comparison runs the other way. Here are the two positions, written as descriptions (Figure~\ref{fig:cut-argument}).

\emph{Description One: select this candidate.} Identifying one particular complete output takes a program or an address that picks it out, and that is a cut: it carries the information that separates \emph{this} from everything else. Nobody knows how long a description of your universe would be, and the length depends on the chosen machine. It is not free.

\emph{Description Two: enumerate every program.} An enumerator can be written compactly: \emph{dovetail all programs and record their outputs}.\footnote{``Generator'' is metaphor. Program space is a mathematical object; the pseudocode is our compact description of it, not evidence that a cosmic computer runs it in time.} Its description is short on ordinary reference machines.

So the two costs come apart. Counted in entities, enumeration is the extravagant position. Counted in description, it is the cheap one, and selection is what costs. Read as a rule about description length, the razor does not obviously favor the single selected world.

Code length is not ontological parsimony. Applying description length to existence is a further step, and the comparison depends on the language in which the positions are written. The observation motivates the postulate; it does not prove it. The book takes the postulate for the reason already given: it declines to treat any decomposition as the structure's own.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

The slip has been exposed. Reading a weight on candidate programs as a weight on realities draws a boundary the formula does not draw; it is a cut, made quietly. The postulate puts a smaller move in its place. It refuses to crown any cut, the system you call the world included, and it supplies no measure on realities in exchange.

What is real, then, is not answered here. It is only no longer answered in advance.
