\chapter*{Further Reading}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{\protect\numberline{}Further Reading}

This is not a bibliography. It is a list of the books that stand behind the one you just read, grouped by what they will do for you. If you want the mathematics at the level of theorems, Li and Vitányi's \textit{An Introduction to Kolmogorov Complexity and Its Applications} is the standard reference and far friendlier than its title; everything technical in this book is treated there with care. The books below are for other kinds of depth.

\section*{The mathematics, at the source}

\textbf{Ray Solomonoff, ``A Formal Theory of Inductive Inference''} (1964). The two-part paper where the universal prior first appears. Dense, but the idea that starts everything in Part I is right there on the page: prediction as a weighting of every program by its length.

\textbf{Ming Li and Paul Vitányi, \textit{An Introduction to Kolmogorov Complexity and Its Applications}} (1993, with later editions). The standard text, and the one place to find every theorem this book leans on: Kraft, the invariance theorem, dominance, all of it. Rigorous and, against the odds, readable.

\textbf{Gregory Chaitin, \textit{Meta Math!}} (2005). The randomness side of the same mathematics, told by one of its inventors, with an enthusiasm that is its own argument.

\section*{Optimal minds}

\textbf{Marcus Hutter, \textit{Universal Artificial Intelligence}} (2005). Solomonoff induction turned into an agent: AIXI, the optimal learner and actor, uncomputable and exact. The technical heart of what Chapters 5 and 9 only gesture at. Demanding, and worth it.

\section*{The computable multiverse}

\textbf{Max Tegmark, \textit{Our Mathematical Universe}} (2014). The fullest popular case for taking the multiverse of mathematical structures as real. Tegmark is warmer and more hopeful about it than this book; read him for the vision, then decide how much of it you will pay for.

\textbf{David Deutsch, \textit{The Fabric of Reality}} (1997). Computation, multiverse, and knowledge braided together by someone who thinks the branches are where everything important happens. Broad, confident, generative.

\textbf{Jürgen Schmidhuber, ``A Computer Scientist's View of Life, the Universe, and Everything''} (1997). The short paper that put the dovetailing generator and the speed prior on the table. The Library, run as a program.

\section*{The self, dissolved}

\textbf{Derek Parfit, \textit{Reasons and Persons}} (1984). The most careful book ever written on personal identity, and the one that matters most once you take many indices seriously. What survives a copy is exactly Parfit's question, reached here by another road. Chapter 14 is his.

\textbf{Thomas Metzinger, \textit{The Ego Tunnel}} (2009). The self as a model the brain runs and cannot see through. If the dissolution in Chapter 14 unsettled you, Metzinger finishes the job.

\section*{Living inside the structure}

\textbf{Albert Camus, \textit{The Myth of Sisyphus}} (1942). The source of the book's closing move. The indifference is given; the rock is given; the only question is what to do with the climb. \textit{Il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux.}

\textbf{Simone Weil, \textit{Gravity and Grace}} (1947). Attention as the rarest and purest form of generosity. Weil understood the indifference of the structure and chose to attend to the people inside it.

\section*{The Library in literature}

\textbf{Jorge Luis Borges, ``The Library of Babel'' and ``The Garden of Forking Paths''} (1941). The totality and the branching, as literature, decades before the mathematics named either. The first is this book's central image; the second belongs to \textit{Multitudes}. Each is short enough for one sitting.

\section*{The rest of the argument}

\textbf{Alex Towell, \textit{Worldlines: The Indifference of Geometry}} (2026) and \textbf{\textit{Multitudes: The Indifference of Measure}} (2026). The first two volumes, by the other two roads. Relativity and quantum mechanics, taken literally, arrive where this book arrives: an indifferent structure, and the warmth we owe each other inside it. Either can be read alone; the three together are one argument.
