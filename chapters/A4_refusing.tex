\chapter{Refusing the Postulate}\label{app:refusing}

The book asks for exactly one thing that ordinary reasoning does not already hand you: the postulate of Chapter 6, that no cut is privileged. Chapter 9 said, near its end, that a reader could refuse the postulate and keep the bedrock. This appendix says plainly what that refusal costs, and what it does not.

Start with what survives. Refuse the postulate entirely, and nothing in the bedrock moves. Bayes' theorem is unchanged: it is the only way to combine a prior and a likelihood that respects the axioms of probability, and it carries no metaphysical reading of what results. Kraft's inequality is unchanged: the codeword lengths of a prefix-free code sum to at most one whether or not you think the codes describe anything real. The universal prior $M$ is unchanged as an object: a well-defined distribution over strings, built from program lengths alone. The dominance theorem, that $M$ catches up to any computable distribution up to a constant, is a theorem about programs and probability, proved without reference to the postulate. The invariance theorem, that the choice of universal machine matters only up to a constant, holds the same way. None of these results mentions the postulate in its statement or needs it in its proof. You can accept every page of Part I, use $M$ to predict, trust Solomonoff's optimality, and never once ask whether any of it is more than a fact about reasoning. Prediction, compression, and the simplicity bias survive a refusal completely intact, because they never rested on it in the first place.

What you give up is the ontological reading of Parts II and III. Chapter 6 took one further step past the bedrock: reading $M$ not only as a measure over what you should believe but as a measure over what is. Refuse that step and you keep a single actual world, the one you are in, real in the ordinary sense, and a self that is simply you, not a carving among equally uncarved alternatives. The dissolutions that follow, the index that turns out to have no privileged answer, the system that turns out to be one carving among cuts the structure ranks equally, the self that turns out to be a local pattern rather than a fixed point, do not go through without that further step, because each rests on reading the universal prior ontically rather than epistemically. What remains is a powerful, well-founded theory of prediction married to an ordinary metaphysics: one world, one you, uncertain about a great deal but not adrift among uncounted, equally real versions of yourself. That is a coherent place to stand, and nothing in the bedrock forces you off it.

The book does not argue you out of that place. Chapter 6 showed what privileging your world costs, in description length, and what declining to privilege it buys: multiplicity, and a cleaner account of the simplicity bias. It does not show that the cost is one you are obligated to refuse. Keeping the ordinary metaphysics is not a failure of nerve and not a misreading; it is a different cut, made honestly, at a fork the book insists on naming rather than smoothing over. If you have read this far and still prefer the world you started with, you have understood the book, not missed it. Take the bedrock. Leave the rest, or do not. Either way, nothing here asked you to take anything on faith. It only asked you to notice, once, that a choice was being made.
