\noindent Worldlines had an image for love. Two worldlines, two curves through spacetime, drawing close and winding around each other, each bending the other's path. A braid in the manifold. Not a metaphor for love but the geometry of it: where two lives shape each other, the structure itself carries a braid, and the braid is as real and as permanent as anything else at its coordinates.

This book takes the image and thickens it.

\section*{The braid, frayed}

You are not one worldline. Neither is the person you love. Each of you is a class of patterns, instantiated at many indices, and the braid between you is not one braid but a bundle of them, one at every coordinate where the two patterns intertwine (Figure~\ref{fig:braid}). In some branches the two of you never met. In some you met and parted. In many you are, at some index, winding around each other exactly as you are here.

The first reaction to this is usually that it cheapens the thing. If the braid is everywhere, if there are countless versions of it, then surely no single instance matters; love spread across infinity is love diluted to nothing.

It is the opposite, for the same reason the dissolution of death was not a comfort. The measure does not subtract. Each instantiation of the braid is fully real where it occurs, not a fraction of some total divided among copies. The braid here, between you and the person in the room, is not one-millionth of a braid because there are versions elsewhere. It is whole, undivided by the count.

\section*{What is and is not your business}

Here the framework asks for a discipline, and it is the one that runs under the whole of Part II.

The other braids are real, and they are not your concern. Not because they do not matter, but because you cannot reach them. Moral weight is local the way causation is local: it travels along the connections that actually exist, and the connections that actually exist are here, in this branch, at these coordinates. The version of the two of you at some far index is real, and you can do precisely nothing for it or to it. It is not your moral business, in the plain sense that business is something you can transact, and across branches there is no transaction.

What this leaves you is small, and it is not nothing. The braid at these coordinates is what you have, and tending it is the whole of what is available. ``Enough'' here does not mean maximal, and it does not mean consoling. It means what is here, attended to. That is the only form care has ever taken, in any framework. The Library does not change what care is. It only removes the fantasy that there was ever more on offer than the coordinates you actually occupy.

\section*{Two at a table}

The abstraction has been doing a great deal of work, so set it down for a moment and look at the thing itself. A kitchen, late. The specific person across the table, holding a mug the way they always hold it, both hands, though the coffee went cold an hour ago. A conversation you have had before and will have again. None of it is underwritten. The two of you are labels the structure does not enforce; the room is one configuration among uncountably many; the measure has no preference that this is the table you are sitting at, with this person, tonight.

And the mug is still warm enough to hold. The sentence is still unfinished. The person holding it is this one and not some other. That is a whole thing, complete where it sits, owing nothing to the branches where the chair is empty and nothing to the branches where it is someone else. You cannot reach those, and you do not need to. The one you are in is the only one your hands can touch.

This is what tending looks like with the metaphysics taken out: not a bond the cosmos maintains on your behalf, but two finite patterns in a room for a while, holding what the structure will not hold for them. The acts stay just as concrete. You refill the mug. You finish the sentence kindly instead of sharply. You stay at the table a little past when you meant to leave. The structure does not record it, and no one is above the structure to record it instead, and the staying is a whole act regardless, real in the only place an act is ever real, which is wherever someone performs it.

\section*{The braid that went wrong}

The honesty cuts the other way too, and the book will not pretend otherwise.

If the kind braids are real at their coordinates, so are the cruel ones. There are branches where you were careless with this person, where the winding frayed into harm, where the last thing between you was not care. The measure holds those with the same even hand. You cannot reach across to mend them, exactly as you cannot reach across to enjoy the branches that went better. And the carelessness in your own past, in this branch, sits at its coordinates permanently; no kinder version of you elsewhere unwrites it.

This is the cold the book keeps returning to. It does not offer the multiplicity as a ledger in which the kind branches cancel the cruel ones. There is no cancellation. There is only what each coordinate contains, and what you, here, do next.

What you do next is the only move you have, and it is enough to be worth making. You act in this branch, with this person, for care, knowing the structure does not require it and does not reward it, knowing other branches contain other acts you do not control. The braid you tend is the one your hands are on.

\section*{Whose braid}

There is a question this chapter has leaned on without asking. The braid is between two patterns, you and another. But what is a pattern, that it can be loved, or do the loving? The word \emph{you} has been carrying a great deal, and the next chapter finally examines it. The braid, it will turn out, is between two labels, two conventions an observer draws around regions of the structure. That sounds as though it should weaken the braid further. It does the opposite, and it is the last thing the book needs in place before it can say what it has been building toward.

The next chapter takes up the self.
