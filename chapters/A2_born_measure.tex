\chapter{The Born Measure and the Universal Prior}\label{app:born}

Chapter 8 named the Born measure and the universal prior as one kind of object, seen twice. This appendix says exactly what that identification claims, and exactly what it does not.

Start with what each measure does. Quantum mechanics, read without a collapse postulate, produces branches: a system that passes through a superposition has its wave function evolve into decoherent outcomes, and each branch carries a weight, the Born weight $|\psi|^2$. Algorithmic information theory produces programs: for a universal prefix machine $U$, the universal prior $M$ (Appendix~\ref{app:bedrock}) assigns each output string the combined weight of every program that produces it, $2^{-|p|}$ summed over the programs $p$ that output it. In both settings the object doing the work is a measure: a way of weighting the many ways some totality of possibility divides into branches, or into descriptions. Neither measure is a mechanism. The Born rule does not push branches into existence with more or less force, any more than $M$ writes some programs into being and leaves others unwritten. Both are bookkeeping. Once the totality is given (all branches, all programs), the measure records how much weight a given carving deserves. This is the precise sense in which the two are the same kind of object: both are the accounting attached to a cut, never the cut's cause, and never a preference the structure itself holds.

That much is a structural observation, and it is the whole of what this book claims. It is worth being just as precise about what it does not claim.

It does not derive the Born rule from $M$. No calculation in this book, or reachable from its bedrock, starts from the axioms of algorithmic information theory and outputs $|\psi|^2$ rather than, say, a flat count of branches or some other weighting entirely. The two measures arise from different formal settings, unitary evolution in one case, program length in the other, and nothing here shows that one entails the other. A reader hoping this appendix supplies the missing derivation of the Born rule, the ``why this weighting and not another'' that has stood open since the 1920s, will not find it, because it is not being offered.

What the book does instead is relocate the question. ``Why the Born measure, and not some other weighting of the branches?'' sounds like a question with a determinate answer waiting to be found, if only the right theory were known. But under the postulate of Chapter 6, that no cut is privileged, a weighting of branches is a cut like any other. To ask why the true weighting is this one presupposes a standpoint from which ``this weighting, and not that one'' already has a settled sense: a vantage the postulate says does not exist. The question does not get answered here. It gets shown to rest on an assumption, that some vantage adjudicates among weightings, that the rest of the book has already declined. What is left is not a mystery solved but a mystery correctly placed: not ``why this measure,'' but ``why the demand for a privileged measure felt like it needed a privileged answer in the first place.''

This is also the precise seam to \textit{Multitudes}, Volume II of this suite, the companion book that takes quantum branching seriously as physics. \textit{Multitudes} postulates the Born measure and builds forward from it without explaining why it, and not some alternative, is the weighting nature uses; that silence is deliberate there, the way a low-entropy past is postulated without explanation in \textit{Worldlines}. This book does not fill that silence either. It only names what kind of silence it is: not a gap in the physics waiting on better theory, but the same gap that opens wherever a measure is asked to justify its own privilege. \textit{Multitudes} need not be read first; the gloss above is the whole of what this book borrows from it, and the whole of what it hands back.
