\noindent \textsc{Chapter 4 gave you a prior.} It came from a counting argument on prefix-free codes: each program weighted by $2^{-|p|}$, summed over all programs producing the output $x$. The universal prior $M(x)$.

You can use $M$. You can also use something else.

The reason to use $M$ is a theorem.

\section*{The dominance theorem}

Let $\mu$ be any computable probability distribution on output strings: any distribution that an algorithm can sample from, or compute the probability of.\footnote{The theorem in fact holds for the wider class of lower-semicomputable semimeasures, which is the natural home of $M$ itself; ``computable distribution'' is the friendly special case, and the one every example below belongs to.} Most distributions of practical interest are computable. Gaussian, exponential, geometric. Every distribution a statistician has written down. Every distribution a physics calculation can produce.

The dominance theorem says: there is a constant $c_\mu$ such that

$$M(x) \geq c_\mu \cdot \mu(x)$$

for every string $x$.

In words: for each such $\mu$, $M$ puts at least a fixed fraction of $\mu$'s weight on every string. That fraction can be tiny.

The constant is positive. It does not depend on $x$; it depends on $\mu$ and on the chosen universal machine.

\section*{Why this is true}

The proof is a few lines.

Since $\mu$ is computable, there is a program that reads random bits and generates a sequence distributed according to $\mu$. A shortest description of that generator has length $K(\mu)$ relative to the chosen machine, with fixed interpreter overhead.

That generator contributes a fixed share of $M$. For any prefix $x$, the fair random inputs that make it begin with $x$ have total weight $\mu(x)$. Encoding the generator costs $K(\mu)+O(1)$ bits, so its share is at least $2^{-K(\mu)-O(1)}\mu(x)$.

Other programs may also contribute, only adding to the total. Therefore

$$M(x) \geq 2^{-K(\mu)-O(1)} \cdot \mu(x).$$

Figure~\ref{fig:dominance-proof} shows the inequality: the $\mu$-sampler's contribution alone, beside the full $M(x)$.

The construction gives a bound of the form $c_\mu \gtrsim 2^{-K(\mu)}$, with machine-dependent overhead. Distributions that are short to describe can receive a better guarantee; long descriptions can make the guaranteed fraction extremely small. But every lower-semicomputable semimeasure is represented with some positive constant.

\section*{What this means for prediction}

Solomonoff prediction conditions sequential $M$ on observations (Figure~\ref{fig:sequential-prediction}). The observed prefix is fed to $M$; conditional ratios give weights for the next symbol. If the data are generated by a computable probability measure, standard Solomonoff convergence results say these predictions approach the source conditional with probability one, in the senses made precise by the theorem.

The bound on cumulative log-loss comes directly from the dominance theorem. Take logarithms of the inequality above:

$$\log_2 \mu(x) - \log_2 M(x) \leq K(\mu) + O(1).$$

By the chain rule for log-probabilities, the left side is the cumulative log-loss gap along the sequence. Thus its upper bound is a description-length term plus machine overhead.

The upper bound does not grow with the length of the sequence. The average gap therefore goes to zero. Related bounds control cumulative squared prediction error and yield convergence for computable sources. Figure~\ref{fig:dominance} shows the guarantee schematically.

This is the central guarantee. Under a computable source, Solomonoff prediction converges while paying a description-dependent overhead. It is a powerful universality result. It is not a claim that $M$ wins at every finite time or under every possible criterion of optimality.

\section*{The choice of machine}

The construction of $M$ depended on a choice of universal Turing machine $U$. A different machine $U'$ gives a different prior $M'$.

The invariance theorem says how the choices are related.

For any two universal Turing machines $U$ and $U'$, there exists a constant $c$ such that

$$M(x) \leq c \cdot M'(x) \quad \text{and} \quad M'(x) \leq c \cdot M(x)$$

for all $x$, allowing different constants in the two directions. The constants are determined by compiler overhead between the machines. Mathematics supplies no general ``few hundred bits'' bound for machines chosen in advance.

So universal machines define a multiplicative-equivalence class. This relation supports asymptotic robustness in the computable-source setting. At finite scales, however, the constants may dominate, and a perversely chosen universal machine can encode perverse prior preferences.

There is therefore no single machine-independent prior. There is a family of universal priors with strong asymptotic relations and potentially important finite differences. In any specific construction, you choose a $U$ and keep the choice visible.

\section*{The bedrock, complete}

The bedrock of inference under uncertainty is now in place. Four ingredients:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Cox-style representation results.} Under stated richness and regularity assumptions, coherent plausibilities can be represented by probability.
\item \textbf{Bayes' theorem.} Conditional probability determines the update once a probability model and prior are supplied.
\item \textbf{Kraft plus fair program bits.} Kraft makes the weights $2^{-|p|}$ admissible for prefix-free programs; fair bits choose those weights and define $M$.
\item \textbf{Dominance, convergence, and invariance.} $M$ dominates lower-semicomputable semimeasures up to source- and machine-dependent constants; its predictions converge for computable sources; different universal machines remain multiplicatively related.
\end{itemize}

The results are settled mathematics when their hypotheses and definitions are kept attached. Together they make $M$ a distinguished ideal for universal sequence prediction. They do not prove a unique rational prior or rule out every learner that performs better on a particular source, finite sample, or loss function.

Notice what this does and does not rule out. The preference for short programs in $M$ is not forced by Kraft: it comes from the fair-bit construction. Dominance gives that construction a remarkable guarantee, but other coherent priors and successful specialized learners remain. Invariance limits machine dependence asymptotically without erasing it. Ask ``why consider simple explanations?'' and the honest answer is cumulative: compression is useful to finite agents, fair program bits provide a clean universal mixture, and the mixture has dominance and convergence theorems. That is a powerful case for \emph{a} universal ideal, not a proof of \emph{the} uniquely rational prior.

Hold onto how strong this is, and how narrow. These are facts about a constructed predictor under stated assumptions. Finitude forces an observer to approximate, but it does not force this prior. Reading program weights as facts about what exists would be a separate interpretive move. The next chapter examines that temptation and names the postulate the book actually adopts.

\section*{The bedrock in the wild}

This is mathematics from the 1960s. Sixty years ago, it was an abstract result, of interest mainly to a handful of researchers in computability and information theory.

It is no longer abstract.

Modern language models make the analogy vivid, but only as an analogy. They are finite next-token predictors trained to reduce cross-entropy on text. Cross-entropy is a data code length under the model; minimum description length would also charge for specifying the model itself. Lower loss means better prediction on the evaluated distribution, not measured closeness to Solomonoff's $M$.

Larger models can represent richer families of distributions, and empirical scaling laws often show smoother improvement with compute and data. Solomonoff dominance does not predict those curves: real training depends on architecture, optimization, sampling, and a fixed resource budget absent from the ideal theorem. The shared shape is universal prediction under uncertainty, nothing stronger.

Acting has a related idealization. Hutter introduced AIXI in 2000 and developed it at book length in 2005: an uncomputable agent that combines a universal mixture with expected-reward maximization. Its claims depend on the chosen universal machine, reward and horizon, and the relevant optimality notion. Reinforcement-tuned language models do not thereby become AIXI approximations; the formal objectives and planning procedures differ.

Some failures of these systems resemble Russell's chicken: confident extrapolation meets a distribution shift. Others come from optimization, representation, data, or objective mismatch. The analogy illustrates bounded induction; it does not certify the systems as approximations to $M$ or their reasoning as sound.

The contemporary systems make sequential prediction culturally visible. They do not empirically confirm the dominance theorem, which is already a mathematical result about a different idealized object.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

The mathematical core is in place: Bayes' theorem, a universal construction, dominance and convergence results, and machine-relative invariance. The distinctions among them matter as much as the formulas.

What remains is interpretation.

So far, the universal semimeasure has weighted candidate generative descriptions of sequential data. The next chapter examines the temptation to read those weights ontologically, a step the mathematics does not force.
