\chapter{The Pattern}

\chapterepigraph[0.6]
{[The self is] nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement.}
{David Hume, \textit{A Treatise of Human Nature} (1739)}

\noindent \textsc{This chapter is about the one reading it}, the one every page so far has called \emph{you}.

\section*{The temporal dissolution, recalled}

\textit{Worldlines} already took the self apart once, along the time axis. On its block-universe reading, a person is a worldline, a four-dimensional shape, and the five-year-old and the eighty-year-old are equally real cross-sections of it. There is no thin moving spark of selfhood travelling down the curve; there is the whole curve, and \emph{I} is the word a cross-section uses for the shape it belongs to. The self was not destroyed by this. It was relocated, from a moving point to a static pattern.

This book takes the self apart a second time, along a different axis. The question is no longer \emph{when} the self is, but where its edge falls, and who drew it there.

\section*{The self as a local model}

Recall the working model from Chapter 8: an observer builds tractable local models of its surroundings, including a model of itself. We can represent that self-model by a program $p_{\text{self}}$, a compressed and useful approximation of the person and situation. It helps the system predict its next move, hold a history, and say \emph{I}. It is not a further thing seated inside the observer. It is a description, not the whole of what it describes.

The model is good enough that you rarely catch it working. You catch it at the edges. Reach for the light switch in a dark house you have lived in for years and your hand finds it, because the model of your arm and the model of the wall agree. Move house, and for a month your hand reaches for the old wall. Or take an experiment psychologists have run since the late 1990s. Hide a person's real hand behind a screen, set a rubber hand on the table in front of them, and stroke both with small brushes, in time. Within minutes many people report that the rubber hand feels like theirs, and their bodies react when it is threatened. The boundary of \emph{my body}, which feels like the most found thing in the world, is drawn and redrawn by the model doing the feeling.

Two thinkers reached this without the Library. Derek Parfit argued that personal identity is not a deep further fact: what there is, is a stream of mental states with more or less connectedness, and ``the same person'' is a way of summarizing the connectedness, not a thread running underneath it. Thomas Metzinger argued that the self is a model the brain runs and cannot see through, a representation so transparent the system mistakes it for a thing. Both land where the working model lands: the self is a compression, useful, finite, and not the structure it compresses.

\section*{One cut, three times}

Here are the three depths of the cut side by side, each carved two ways: the index you make to predict, the system you call the world, and the self that does the cutting. The claim that these are one move, not three, is worth something only if you can watch the move happen.

\emph{The index, carved twice.} Go back to the coin of Chapter 3. Ten thousand trials, five flips each, and about $409$ of them came up HHHHH. You sorted those $409$ into two bins, trick and fair, counted, and found about $24\%$ in the first. Chapter 3 said it at the time: the counting was exact, but the bins were not in the trials; they came with your question. Weights come after cuts. Ask a different one (was the coin minted before 1990? did the left hand or the right do the flipping?) and the same $409$ trials sort into different bins, and the same exact counting returns a different answer, just as correct. Ask nothing and there are no bins at all: $409$ particular runs of five particular flips, each its own event, nothing in them grouping one with another. The data restricted the count. What it was a count \emph{of} was yours.

\emph{The system, carved twice.} Now go back to the kitchen of Chapter 13. Read it the way you read it at the time: a table, a chair, a mug of cold coffee, a person. Four things, cleanly edged. Now read the same room the way physics reads it: one continuous patch of matter and field, denser here and thinner there. At that grain there is no line where the mug stops and the hand begins, only a region where one kind of molecule thins out and another thickens. Both readings work. The first is the one you need to refill a mug; the second is the one you need to explain why the coffee went cold. Neither is the room's own. The world you call \emph{this one} is the same kind of reading, done at the scale of everything you can observe.

\emph{The self, carved twice, and then again.} You have drawn this line before. In Chapter 11 you drew it around the one who chose, tight around a decision and then wide enough to take in a family; in Chapter 12, around the one who dies. Each time a job fixed where it went: answering for a choice, naming a loss. Now take the job away and draw a line around \emph{you}. The obvious place is the skin: inside is me, outside is the world. The rubber hand has already shown that this line is drawn by a model, not found. Draw it instead around a body and its history: the five-year-old, the person you were at twenty, the one reading this, joined by memory and habit and one continuous track through spacetime. That is the line \textit{Worldlines} drew, and it includes people you could not now hold a conversation with. Or draw it around a relationship. There is a version of you that exists only at that kitchen table, that speaks in a register nobody else hears and carries a history nobody else holds. When the other person dies, that version does not survive intact in either of you; people who have grieved say a part of them went too, and they are not speaking loosely. Each of these lines picks out something real: a body, a history, a braid. Each is the right line for some job. Parfit's suggestion was that when the lines come apart, asking which one is \emph{really} you may be an empty question, and that what matters (the memories, the commitments, the love) is carried by the connections, whichever line you draw around them.

Figure~\ref{fig:three-cuts} puts the three lines on one field. The field does not change. The lines are nested, and one hand draws all of them, for three jobs: \emph{I} to act, \emph{here} to predict, \emph{this world} to have laws and objects to predict with.

Who am I? Where am I? What world is this? Each question asks for a boundary, and, on the postulate, each boundary is drawn by the one asking. The self is a cut; so is the index; so is the system you call the world. This is the cut Chapter 2 said you would meet three times, and it was one cut all along. There are no joints. There are only the cuts you make. One of them is you.

\section*{What is preserved}

This is not annihilation, and it is worth being exact about why. What matters was never riding on a further fact underneath the connections; it rides on them, which is Parfit's point. Carry that into the Library and one more conclusion follows, one that needs no occurrence measure: no thread supplied by $M$ ties ``you here'' to a hypothetical ``you there,'' which is Chapter 9's center again, seen from inside, and what matters here does not need one. What dissolves is only the demand that the line around you be the structure's own, correct from every view.

If this lands as vertigo rather than relief, hold it lightly. That the self is a cut is a claim about a boundary and a model, not a claim that the person or the experience is unreal. You are here.

\section*{Why this lets care be care}

Chapter 13 found that \emph{we} is a cut you make anyway. The self adds one thing more. The line around you is drawn too, and so is the line around the person across the table, so nothing on either side holds the relation up: no thread tying you to yourself, none tying you to them, for a relationship to coast on. A braid between two cuts is held together by nothing but the tending: memory kept up, attention paid, the repair after the bad night, the same small acts done again. The dissolution of a canonical self turns care from a thing you possess into a thing you do.

That is the last piece. The book can now say what it has been building toward.
