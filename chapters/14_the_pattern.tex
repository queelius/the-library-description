\noindent The word \emph{you} has done a great deal of work in this book, and it is time to look at what it names.

\section*{The temporal dissolution, recalled}

\textit{Worldlines} already took the self apart once, along the time axis. A person is a worldline, a four-dimensional shape, and the five-year-old and the eighty-year-old are equally real cross-sections of it. There is no thin moving spark of selfhood travelling down the curve; there is the whole curve, and \emph{I} is the word a cross-section uses for the shape it belongs to. The self was not destroyed by this. It was relocated, from a moving point to a static pattern.

This book takes the self apart a second time, along a different axis.

\section*{The self as a local model}

Recall what an observer turned out to be (Chapter 8): a substructure that builds short local models of its surroundings, including a model of itself. That self-model is a program like the others, a $p_{\text{self}}$, a compressed and useful approximation of the region of structure the observer occupies. It is what lets the system predict its own next move, hold a history, say \emph{I}. It is not a further thing seated inside the observer. It is the observer's most-used local theory, and like every theory it is a description, not the thing described.

Two thinkers reached this without the Library. Derek Parfit argued that personal identity is not a deep further fact: what there is, is a stream of mental states with more or less connectedness, and ``the same person'' is a way of summarizing the connectedness, not a thread running underneath it. Thomas Metzinger argued that the self is a model the brain runs and cannot see through, a representation so transparent the system mistakes it for a thing. Both land where Chapter 8 landed: the self is a compression, useful, finite, and not the structure it compresses.

\section*{The dissolution across realities}

Now run the model through the Library.

The pattern your self-model approximates is instantiated at many indices. The label \emph{I} picks out a region of the measure, the region where configurations carry the memory-structure, the sensory style, the continuity of attention the model tracks. It does not pick out one of those configurations as the true bearer of the name. There is no fact, in the structure, about which instantiation is \emph{really} you, for the same reason there was no fact about which moment of the worldline was \emph{really} now. \emph{I} is a label on a region, drawn by the model the region runs.

And here the deepest thread of the book closes. The two questions that have haunted it, \emph{who am I} and \emph{where am I}, turn out to be one question with the same shape of answer. The self is a compression the observer brings to a continuous structure. So is the index, the coordinate, the very notion of a discrete ``where'' in the Library. Both are labels a finite observer attaches to a structure that has none of its own. Dissolve the self and you dissolve, in the same motion, the privileged coordinate it was standing on. There is no unique you, and no unique here, because both were the same act of compression, performed at two levels.

\section*{What is preserved}

This is not annihilation, and it is worth being exact about why.

Parfit's move was to separate identity from what matters. You may not be the same entity, in any deep sense, as the person who went to sleep last night; the connectedness is what there is, and it is enough to carry everything you actually care about, your memories, your commitments, the people you love. Identity was never doing the work you thought it was. What matters is the pattern and its connections, and those are real wherever they occur.

Carry that into the Library and it holds, with the indifference turned up. Identity is not preserved across indices; no thread ties ``you here'' to ``you there.'' But what matters, the features, the connectedness, the braid, is fully present at every coordinate where it occurs. You do not need the unique self in order to have everything the unique self was supposed to guarantee. You only thought you did.

\section*{Why this lets care be care}

There is a consequence the last chapter was waiting for.

If the self is a convention, a label drawn around a region, then so is the person you love, and so is the braid between you: a relation between two conventions. This sounds as though it should be the final dilution, the point where care evaporates into bookkeeping about labels. It is the reverse.

A braid between two metaphysically guaranteed selves would be a fixed fact, maintained by the structure whether or not anyone tended it. A braid between two conventions is held together by nothing but the tending. The labels do not keep themselves; the connectedness does not persist on its own; the structure has no stake in it. Which means care is not a fact about persons that the cosmos underwrites. It is a practice, ours, performed at these coordinates, holding together a pattern that nothing else will hold. The dissolution of the self is exactly what turns care from a thing you have into a thing you do.

That is the last piece. The book can now say what it has been building toward.

The next chapter is the last.
