\chapter{The Chooser}

\chapterepigraph
{A man can do what he wills, but he cannot will what he wills.}
{after Arthur Schopenhauer, \textit{On the Freedom of the Will} (1839)}

\noindent \textsc{Sometime in the next hour you will decide something small.} Whether to answer a message. Whether to get up for a glass of water. Whether to keep reading past the end of this paragraph. You will weigh it, briefly or hardly at all, and then one thing will happen instead of another.

Ask a plain question about that moment. Where is the one who decides?

The last chapter put the word \emph{I} among the cuts: a boundary drawn around a region for the work of getting around, not a line the structure draws for you. This chapter tests that claim where it seems hardest to believe, at the moment you choose.

\section*{Where the chooser is}

Try drawing the boundary.

Draw it tight, around the decision itself: a few hundred milliseconds of activity in one brain, a competition among signals that ends with one of them winning. That is a chooser. A neuroscientist can study it, time it, and sometimes predict it, better than chance, before you are aware of having decided.

Draw it around the brain, with its habits and its fatigue: the sleep you did not get, the second coffee, the sugar in your blood. That is a chooser too, a slightly different one. It explains why you snap at people at four in the afternoon.

Draw it around a person with a history: the childhood that taught you caution, the friend whose advice you still hear, the promise you made last year. Most of what you mean by \emph{my decision} lives at this boundary. It is the one a court addresses, and the one a friend forgives.

Draw it wider. Around a family, whose way of handling money or anger you inherited before you could argue with it. Around a culture, which settled long before you were born what would even show up as an option. Each of these is a chooser in the sense some question needs: why this happened, and what would have to change for it to happen differently.

Nothing in the structure marks one of these as \emph{the} chooser. Each is a cut. They are not rival answers competing for a single right one; they are different carvings, made for different work, of one causal process that runs through all of them. This is the postulate of Chapter 6, applied to the one who acts. The chooser is a cut.

Schopenhauer's line at the head of this chapter is what happens if you keep asking. You did what you willed; but who willed the willing? Your history. And who made the history? Your family, your culture, the chemistry of a brain. Each answer pushes the boundary outward, and if you never stop pushing, the chooser grows until it is the whole universe, which is to say no one. The regress is real. You stop it the only way it can be stopped: by drawing a line. The line is yours.

Here is the objection that should be made at once. \emph{Yours?} A line drawn by a chooser presupposes a chooser. The chapter defined the one who decides as a cut, and then needed someone to make the cut; it has put the self back in order to draw the boundary around the self. That is a circle.

It is a circle, and it is the same circle a map is in when it marks the place where the mapmaker stands. No one outside the process draws the line around it. The process draws a line around part of itself, and the drawing is one more thing the process does: the brain that deliberates is the brain that models itself deliberating. \emph{Yours} does not name a small person inside the person, holding the pen. It names this process, here, as against the family and the culture that fed it, and the ordinary sense in which this process can answer for what it did. Chapter 14 draws the three lines on one field and says whose hand holds the pen: one of the lines is around it.

\section*{From inside a fixed structure}

None of this makes the choosing unreal, and the block-universe reading of relativity already shows why.

On that reading, spacetime is a single four-dimensional structure, your life a worldline, a curve through it, every moment fixed at its coordinates. You walk left at the bakery. Your worldline contains the walking-left. There is no coordinate in the manifold at which the walking-left was not what was happening there.

From outside, then, the choice is fixed. From inside, it was open: you did not know what you would do, you weighed it, you decided. Both descriptions hold. This is \emph{compatibilism}, the position that free will and determinism do not contradict each other. Choosing is what a deliberating process looks like from inside. It does not need a power to have done otherwise. It needs the structure to contain deliberation, and it does. The weighing is there, at its coordinates, and without it the walking-left would not be there either. The fixity does not go around your deliberation. It runs through it.

The Library adds one thing to that answer and takes nothing away. Wherever you draw the boundary around the chooser, the deliberation inside it is a real causal process here, and so are its effects. Widening the cut to take in your family does not make your weighing idle; it shows more of what fed it. Narrowing the cut to a few hundred milliseconds does not erase your history; it sets the history outside the frame for a moment. The process is the same process. Only the carving changes.

\section*{Responsibility}

If the chooser is a cut, where does responsibility go?

Where it always went: to a drawn boundary. We already practice this without naming it. We hold a person responsible, and not their neurons. We widen the boundary for a child, or for someone acting under a threat, and say the choosing was partly elsewhere. We widen it again, to an institution, when no one inside it could have done otherwise, and say the hospital failed, or the company. None of these discovers a joint in the structure. Each is a carving made for the work responsibility does: asking for reasons, making repair, doing differently next time.

That work picks the usual boundary. The person with a history is the cut that can hear a reason, answer for what happened, and change. So that is where the line ordinarily goes, and where, in the ordinary case, you should draw it around yourself. That you drew the line does not let you off it; responsibility has never attached to anything else.

The cut can be merciful, too. Some people draw the line around themselves so wide that they own every outcome they were anywhere near, including the ones that were never in their reach. Seeing the boundary as drawn lets you redraw it where your hands actually were.

\section*{One optional lens}

Chapter 8 met Everett's reading of quantum mechanics, often called the many-worlds interpretation, as a landmark and not as evidence. If you hold that reading, a decision can be represented as decoherent continuations in which different things happen. Nothing in this chapter needs it. The deliberation is still a causal process here, the boundary around the chooser is still a cut you draw, and other continuations, if there are any, do not grade the choice made here.

\section*{Quantum immortality}

That lens carries one idea with it, and the idea circulates, so it should be named. Suppose every event that could end a life also has outcomes, on Everett's reading, in which the person survives. No one ever looks back from an outcome they did not survive. So, the argument runs, from the inside experience always goes on, along whichever outcomes still contain the one experiencing it. This is quantum immortality.

It is not a theorem of quantum mechanics, and it is not about quantum mechanics. It needs a supplier of occurrences, which Chapter 9 showed need not be quantum, and the two rules that chapter named: one saying which later moments count as continuing you, and one that spreads first-person weight only over the moments that can report an outcome. The book grants none of the three.

This argument has reached people at their worst moments, so here is the plain version. Nothing in it is a reason to take a risk with your life. The framework permits the argument to be stated; it does not ask you to take it as your situation. If this has stopped being abstract for you, put the book down and turn to \hyperref[resources]{\textit{Resources}, at the back of the book}, now.

Chapter 9 said what the rules are doing, with the infinite cosmos as the case; for the reader who wants the argument taken apart premise by premise, Appendix~\ref{app:anthropics} does that work.

\section*{What the framework tells you about choosing}

Almost nothing.

It tells you that deliberation is part of the causal process, wherever you draw the line around the one deliberating. It tells you that the line is drawn. That is close to all of it.

What it does not say is which choices to make. It does not say whether to take the job or turn it down, whether to make the call or let it ring. It does not say whether to pursue care over cruelty, attention over inattention, the work over avoidance.

The choosing happens here. Your choice changes what follows within its causal reach, and that is enough for responsibility, without a census of anything elsewhere. The mathematics never gets that far.

\section*{Where this leaves you}

You choose. The deliberation is real, its consequences are real, and both are here, whatever else turns out to be true. Where the one who chose begins and ends is a cut, made for the work of answering for the choice, and you are the one who makes it.

Draw it where someone can answer.
