These notes carry no markers in the text. They are keyed by phrase and grouped by chapter, for the reader who wants a source or a small precision the prose had no room for. The few footnotes in the chapters are asides the argument needed on the page; everything else is here.

\section*{Chapter 1: The Question}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item ``The man who has fed the chicken every day throughout its life at last wrings its neck instead'' is Bertrand Russell's own image, from \textit{The Problems of Philosophy} (1912), chapter 6, ``On Induction.''
\item ``David Hume named it in 1748'' points to \textit{An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding}; the same argument appears nine years earlier in \textit{A Treatise of Human Nature} (1739).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 2: The Library}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item ``In 1941, Jorge Luis Borges described a library'' dates the Spanish original, ``La biblioteca de Babel''. English readers meet it in James E. Irby's translation (1962, in \textit{Labyrinths}) or Andrew Hurley's (1998, in \textit{Collected Fictions}, p.~112), which differ in small details of wording. The chapter epigraph follows Hurley; the line facing the contents page follows Irby.
\item ``The address of a book is a book'' uses the story's own figures: 410 pages, 40 lines, 80 characters, 25 orthographic symbols (the narrator counts twenty-two letters, the space, the comma, and the period). The counting argument that follows it is the standard incompressibility argument; Li and Vit\'anyi give it in section 2.2, and it is the reason most strings are, in their phrase, incompressible.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 3: The Indexing}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is from a letter James Clerk Maxwell wrote to Lewis Campbell in 1850, at nineteen; it is printed in Campbell and Garnett, \textit{The Life of James Clerk Maxwell} (1882).
\item The Cox-style representation result begins with R.\,T.\ Cox, ``Probability, Frequency, and Reasonable Expectation,'' \textit{American Journal of Physics} 14 (1946). E.\,T.\ Jaynes, \textit{Probability Theory: The Logic of Science} (2003), gives an influential development. Joseph Halpern's ``Cox's Theorem Revisited'' (1999) explains why richness and regularity assumptions matter. Appendix~\ref{app:bedrock} states the result schematically rather than pretending there is one assumption-free theorem.
\item ``Laplace started from indifference'' and ``Jeffreys looked for priors that would not change when the problem was redescribed'' point to Pierre-Simon Laplace, \textit{Th\'eorie analytique des probabilit\'es} (1812), and Harold Jeffreys, \textit{Theory of Probability} (1939), with the invariant rule set out in his 1946 paper in the \textit{Proceedings of the Royal Society A}.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 4: The Prior}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is from the opening summary of Part I of Ray Solomonoff, ``A Formal Theory of Inductive Inference,'' \textit{Information and Control} 7, no.~1 (1964), p.~1.
\item ``This is Kraft's inequality, proved in 1949'' cites L.\,G.\ Kraft's 1949 MIT thesis; it is sometimes called the Kraft-McMillan inequality, after Brockway McMillan's 1956 extension to all uniquely decodable codes.
\item ``Machines that read and write this way are called \textit{monotone} machines'': the universal semimeasure in this form, summed over minimal programs, is due to A.\,K.\ Zvonkin and L.\,A.\ Levin, \textit{Russian Mathematical Surveys} 25 (1970), building on Solomonoff. Prefix machines and prefix complexity, used for $K$ and for the exact-output $m_V$ of Appendix~\ref{app:bedrock}, are due to Levin and to Gregory Chaitin (\textit{Journal of the ACM}, 1975). Li and Vit\'anyi keep the two apart: monotone machines and $M$ in section 4.5, prefix machines and $m$ in sections 3.1 and 4.3. Appendix~\ref{app:bedrock} states both and the coding theorem for $m$ and $K$.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 5: The Optimality}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is from the abstract of Marcus Hutter, ``On the Existence and Convergence of Computable Universal Priors,'' \textit{Algorithmic Learning Theory} (ALT 2003), arXiv:cs/0305052; ``his'' is Solomonoff. Hutter introduced AIXI in a 2000 technical report and developed the framework in later papers and the 2005 book \textit{Universal Artificial Intelligence}. Jan Leike and Marcus Hutter, ``Bad Universal Priors and Notions of Optimality'' (2015), show how much of AIXI's optimality depends on the chosen universal machine.
\item ``What this says to the chicken'' reads Solomonoff's bound the standard way. Hutter, ``On Universal Prediction and Bayesian Confirmation,'' \textit{Theoretical Computer Science} 384 (2007), discusses how the result bears on Hume's problem and what it still assumes about the source.
\item The invariance theorem is associated with Solomonoff, Kolmogorov, and Chaitin, who developed related versions in the 1960s. Machine dependence at finite scales is substantive, not merely cosmetic.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 8: The Observer}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is Bryce DeWitt, ``Quantum Mechanics and Reality,'' \textit{Physics Today} 23 (1970); the branching reading traces to Hugh Everett III's original paper (\textit{Reviews of Modern Physics} 29, 1957).
\item For decoherence and the limits of what it establishes, see Maximilian Schlosshauer, ``Decoherence, the measurement problem, and interpretations of quantum mechanics,'' \textit{Reviews of Modern Physics} 76 (2005). Decoherence explains effective suppression of interference; it does not by itself select an ontology or a unique outcome.
\item ``how many branches there are depends on how finely you cut'': David Wallace, \textit{The Emergent Multiverse} (2012), argues the point from within the Everettian view.
\item ``The Self-Sampling Assumption (SSA)'' and ``The Self-Indication Assumption (SIA)'' were named and systematized by Nick Bostrom, \textit{Anthropic Bias} (2002). Appendix~\ref{app:anthropics} treats both, xerographic distributions, and the distinct speed prior. ``Xerographic distributions'' are from Mark Srednicki and James Hartle, ``Science in a Very Large Universe,'' \textit{Physical Review D} 81 (2010). ``Sleeping Beauty'' entered the philosophical literature with Adam Elga, ``Self-Locating Belief and the Sleeping Beauty Problem,'' \textit{Analysis} 60 (2000). ``The doomsday argument'' is due to Brandon Carter and was developed by John Leslie, \textit{The End of the World} (1996).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 11: The Chooser}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is the compressed form in which Schopenhauer's thesis is usually quoted, popularized by Albert Einstein in his 1932 credo; Schopenhauer argues it at length in his prize essay \textit{On the Freedom of the Will} (1839).
\item ``A neuroscientist can study it, time it, and sometimes predict it'' refers to the line of experiments begun by Benjamin Libet and colleagues (\textit{Brain}, 1983) and extended by Chun Siong Soon and colleagues (\textit{Nature Neuroscience}, 2008). What those findings show about choice is contested; the chapter needs only that a decision is a process that can be timed.
\item ``This is quantum immortality'' names an argument most associated with Max Tegmark's ``quantum suicide'' thought experiment (\textit{Fortschritte der Physik} 46, 1998), with earlier versions from Hans Moravec (1987) and Bruno Marchal (1988). Appendix~\ref{app:anthropics} takes its premises apart. The chapter's caution applies here too: the thought experiment is not a reason to take any real risk.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 14: The Pattern}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is David Hume, \textit{A Treatise of Human Nature} (1739 to 1740), Book I, Part IV, Section 6, ``Of Personal Identity.''
\item ``Hide a person's real hand behind a screen'' describes the rubber-hand illusion, first reported by Matthew Botvinick and Jonathan Cohen, ``Rubber hands `feel' touch that eyes see,'' \textit{Nature} 391 (1998). The bodily response to a threatened rubber hand was measured by K.\,C.\ Armel and V.\,S.\ Ramachandran, \textit{Proceedings of the Royal Society B} 270 (2003).
\item ``Derek Parfit argued that personal identity is not a deep further fact'' cites Parfit, \textit{Reasons and Persons} (1984), Part III, which is also the source of the suggestion that, when the criteria come apart, which one is \emph{really} you may be an empty question. ``Thomas Metzinger argued that the self is a model the brain runs'' cites Metzinger, \textit{Being No One} (2003), restated for general readers in \textit{The Ego Tunnel} (2009).
\end{itemize}

\section*{Chapter 15: The Pattern Describing Itself}

\begin{itemize}
\setlength{\itemsep}{0.5em}
\item The epigraph is George Eliot, \textit{Middlemarch}, the Finale; 1872 is the completed edition; installments began in December 1871.
\item ``It could decline to treat them as negligible'' is the book's one moral commitment: that no description of suffering is written off, however unreachable. The commitment has old company, which the book has not leaned on and should not be read as borrowing. The universalist strand of Christian theology, from Origen's \textit{apokatastasis} to its recent defenders, holds that no one is finally left outside; the bodhisattva vow of Mahayana Buddhism refuses to be finished until every being is. Those are positions argued from premises this book does not share. Here the refusal rests on nothing but the person making it, and the book says so.
\item ``\textit{il faut imaginer Sisyphe heureux}'' is the closing line of Albert Camus, \textit{Le Mythe de Sisyphe} (1942); Justin O'Brien's standard translation is \textit{The Myth of Sisyphus} (1955).
\end{itemize}
