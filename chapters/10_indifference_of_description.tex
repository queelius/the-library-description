\noindent \textsc{The framework is built in layers.} The distinctions among them are the thesis's guardrails.

Program space contains descriptions of every computable output. Relative to a universal machine, $M$ weights the programs whose outputs begin with your data. The postulate adds that no decomposition into systems, worlds, or selves is view-independently privileged. Neither claim says that every output exists, and neither assigns probabilities to observer occurrences.

This is the book's title.

\section*{Two absences of privilege}

The first belongs to prediction. A finite prefix is compatible with many programs: short and long, lawful and contrived, enduring histories and transient matches. $M$ weights those candidate programs by the fair-bit construction. It need not identify one true generator even when its next-symbol predictions converge. The weights also depend on the reference machine at finite scales.

The second belongs to description. A compact enumerator can list every program without dividing their outputs into one canonical set of worlds, objects, or persons. The postulate says no such division is the structure's own. That is not a frequency claim and not an ontology. It is a refusal to treat one carving as the view from nowhere.

This does not answer why these laws obtain or why quantum experiments follow the Born rule. It does not derive $|\psi|^2$ from program length. The comparison is narrower: both theories use weights after specifying a domain of alternatives. Their formulas, evidential bases, and formal roles remain distinct.

Under the postulate, no representation of quantum alternatives is privileged merely by being a representation. But the scientific question remains: why does this formal rule fit experiments, and how should it be derived or interpreted within a quantum theory? The postulate supplies no shortcut.

Here it helps to separate what the word \emph{simplicity} has been carrying. Kraft makes fair-bit length weights admissible, not compulsory. The fair-bit universal mixture then earns strong dominance and convergence guarantees. Invariance relates universal machines multiplicatively, while finite constants remain consequential. This is a powerful cumulative case for $M$ as an ideal predictor, not a theorem that every rational agent must use one unique prior.

The ontological reading is separate. A successful predictor need not resemble the furniture of reality, and dominance does not say that $M$ tracks every source better than every computable rival at every time. Picture A remains a possible interpretation of program space, not the natural-language restatement of a theorem. A reader can retain all of Part I, accept or refuse the postulate, and choose among several ontologies.

Granting the postulate does not make every \emph{why} above possibility disappear. It tells us that no decomposition answers from nowhere. It leaves the empirical questions, the machine choice, the ontology, and self-location open. Beneath those remains the thinner question of why there is anything describable at all. The book does not answer it.

\section*{The deeper indifference}

A parallel is familiar from physics. Special relativity supplies no observer-independent global present: observers in relative motion slice distant events differently. A block-universe interpretation represents spacetime as a four-dimensional whole with no preferred temporal slice. This is the indifference of geometry developed in the companion volume, \textit{Worldlines}; this book does not assume the reader has met it there.\footnote{The two books form a pair. \textit{Worldlines} works through the indifference of geometry from special relativity; this book works through the indifference of description from algorithmic information theory. Either can be read alone.}

The block-universe reading dissolves a unique global present. Here the postulate dissolves a unique decomposition: no boundary around a system or self is view-independently canonical. That does not prove that many selves or worlds exist. It shows that the labels \emph{system}, \emph{world}, and \emph{I} belong to a descriptive interface, not to $M$ itself.

There is one more distinction from the last chapter. The postulate denies a privileged overview; it does not prove that no powerful or transcendent mind exists. Any proposed mind would still present a view, and greater power would not make its decomposition the structure's own. ``Indifference'' here therefore names the absence of a view-independent selection, not the emotional attitude of a cosmic observer.

\section*{What this is and is not}

The indifference is not an erasure. Your experience now is what it is; the moment of reading these words contains what it contains. Revising a global ontology does not make a local experience disappear.

Nor is it annihilation. A person can be modeled as a pattern without becoming merely fictional. If Picture A contains other matching occurrences, their status and weight remain open; no multiplication is needed to protect the person here.

The label \emph{I} also keeps its meaning. It organizes memory, attention, agency, and responsibility around a locally useful boundary. It need not name a view-independently canonical object to do that work.

Nor does any of this answer \emph{where am I?} as a question among matching occurrences. SSA, SIA, and xerographic distributions propose answers at the price of reference-class choices. The speed prior changes program weights but does not by itself define an occurrence measure. The book adopts none.

The indifference, then, is layered. $M$ assigns weights to candidate programs and prefixes. The postulate denies a privileged decomposition. Ontology and self-location remain additional questions.

\section*{What this does not console}

None of what follows in this book is consolation.

The framework permits descriptions of hellscapes, fluctuation observers, and altered versions of familiar patterns. Picture A may take some or all of those descriptions ontically, but the postulate does not establish that reading, and $M$ supplies no self-location measure over them. The moral difficulty is not erased by this precision; neither is it inflated into a theorem.

The remaining chapters do not argue the possibilities away. They ask a more local question: amid uncertainty about the global picture, what does it look like to be a finite observer with a life, causal reach, and people one loves?

The response that comes is not derived from the mathematics. The mathematics leaves room. The room is what we walk into. Whether what we carry in is enough for what the room holds is not something the book can promise; the last chapter returns to that, and does not close it.

The mathematical theorems stand conditionally on their definitions and hypotheses. What might fail is their application to nature, the choice of universal machine as a useful idealization, the postulate, Picture A, or the Everettian landmark. The response we choose does not depend on which global picture wins.

The thesis is stated. The book's title is on the page.
